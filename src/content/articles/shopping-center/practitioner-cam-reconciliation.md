# CAM Reconciliation for Shopping Centers: The Landlord's Complete Guide

Common-area maintenance is how a shopping center landlord bills tenants for the shared cost of the property: the lot, the landscaping, the lights, the walks, and the management of all of it. Each month the tenant pays an estimate. Once a year the landlord compares estimates to actuals and settles the difference. The shorter series piece, "CAM and NNN Reconciliation: Retail Leasing's Most Misunderstood Work," walks that settlement at overview length. This practitioner reference is the working file: which expenses belong in the pool, how the denominator changes when an anchor is excluded, how three kinds of caps behave, what the statement must show, and how to answer an audit without inventing the story at the end.

Lease language controls. Ranges below are illustrative planning figures, not a market survey and not a statute. Whether a missed deadline waives a right, and whether a tenant may withhold a true-up, is what that tenant's lease says. Reading it is the operator's job. Deciding what a court would do with it is counsel's.

Reconciliation matters for three operating reasons. It is how the landlord collects costs the leases allocated to tenants. It is a standing source of disputes. And it is the once-a-year look at where the money actually went.

## What the lease structure already decided

The structure tells you whether a reconciliation exists at all.

| Lease type | Who pays operating costs | Reconciliation | Where it shows up |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Gross | Landlord, inside a flat rent | No | Some office, some older retail |
| Modified gross | Tenant pays defined costs above a stop | Yes, above the stop | Office and some retail |
| Net | Base rent plus taxes | Taxes only | Some single-tenant deals |
| Double net | Base rent plus taxes and insurance | Those two | Some anchors and ground leases |
| Triple net | Base rent plus taxes, insurance, and CAM | All three | Most inline shopping-center tenants |
| Absolute net | Tenant pays costs including structural repair, as the lease defines them | Whatever the lease includes | Some anchors and ground leases |

Most inline deals at an open-air center are triple net. Anchors often negotiate something else, with carve-outs. That mix is why one center-wide percentage is the wrong bill. You reconcile each lease against its own definition.

## Includable and excludable

Not every dollar the owner spends is recoverable. The lease lists what is in and what is out. This is where landlords over-bill and where they leave money behind.

**Often includable, if the lease says so:** routine lot repair, landscaping and irrigation, exterior lighting, snow and ice where that is a cost, security, a management fee up to a cap, common-area cleaning, trash, pest control, signage maintenance, and property insurance allocated to the common areas.

**Often excludable:** capital expenditures unless the lease allows amortization, depreciation, debt service, leasing commissions, tenant-improvement allowances, the owner's income taxes, costs recovered from insurance or a third party, work that benefits one tenant, and salaries above the management-fee cap. Some leases also exclude costs of vacant space. Read that sentence. It changes the denominator conversation in the next section.

The fight operators see most often is the parking lot. Tenants call a project capital. Landlords call it maintenance. A crack-fill and seal coat is maintenance in ordinary language. A full-depth rebuild is capital. The lease may still allow an amortized piece of the capital job. Document the scope when you hire the work, not when you reconcile it. A proposal, photos, and the invoice are the file. A memory is not.

An illustrative classification, always overruled by the lease in front of you:

| Category | Usually in | Usually out | Note |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Lot maintenance | Routine repair | Full replacement, unless amortized | Scope decides |
| Management fee | Up to the cap | Above the cap | Caps are often a percent of collections or of CAM |
| Capital | Amortized slice, if allowed | The whole cost expensed at once | Useful life or a stated term |
| Insurance | Property and liability for the center | Coverages the lease does not name | Allocate the policy |
| Landscaping | Routine care, irrigation repair | A new installation, if the lease calls it capital | Say which |
| Security | Contracted service | Equipment, unless amortized | Cameras are the usual argument |
| Common utilities | Lot lights and monuments | Tenant-metered suites | Submeters end the argument |

## Pro-rata share and the anchor exclusion

The basic formula is the tenant's square footage divided by a denominator. The denominator is the argument.

**Illustrative center only.** Call it Cedar Crossing: 75,000 square feet of gross leasable area, not a real property. An inline tenant of 2,400 square feet has a share of 2,400 ÷ 75,000 = 3.20% if the denominator is the whole center.

Now exclude a 30,000-square-foot anchor that negotiated out of the denominator. The inline share becomes 2,400 ÷ 45,000 = 5.33%. Every inline tenant's share rises by the same ratio: 75,000 ÷ 45,000 − 1 = 66.7%. The exclusion does not shrink the lot. It moves the anchor's portion of the bill onto everyone else. If that anchor later goes dark, the exclusion is still in the lease. You do not start billing them because the box is empty. Model the exclusion before you buy the center, not after the first reconciliation.

Some leases use occupied area instead of gross leasable area. Vacancy then raises the share of whoever remains. Landlords like that when they are carrying empty space. Tenants like a fixed gross-leasable denominator. In a full center the two numbers are close. In a thin center they are not. Know which one each lease uses. Gross-up clauses, which adjust variable costs toward a stated occupancy, are a separate sentence. Apply them only where the lease allows, and only to costs that actually vary.

## Three caps, and a year with no cap

A cap limits how fast a tenant's CAM can rise. The structures are not interchangeable. Over a ten-year term the difference is money.

| Cap | Applies to | Resets? | Who it tends to favor |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Fixed annual | All CAM, or all capped CAM | Yes, off last year's actual | Tenant, if a down year lowers the base |
| Cumulative | All CAM, compounded off a base year | No | Landlord, if early years ran under the cap |
| Controllable only | Costs the landlord influences | Depends on the lease | Common compromise |
| None | Actual cost | Not applicable | Landlord |

A fixed annual cap of 5% means this year's bill cannot exceed last year's actual by more than 5%. If actual cost falls, next year's ceiling falls with it. A cumulative cap compounds from the first year's base and does not give back a year that came in low. You cannot "catch up" later just because you billed less early.

A controllable cap is the shape many current deals use. Management, landscaping, and similar costs sit under the cap. Taxes, insurance, and utilities pass through at actual cost because the landlord does not set them. The definition of "controllable" is in the lease. Do not assume utilities are out because that is common. Confirm the list.

An expense stop is a different tool, more common in gross and modified-gross deals: the landlord absorbs costs up to a threshold, and the tenant pays the excess. A cap limits the increase. A stop sets a floor the landlord keeps. Do not use the words as synonyms.

## The annual cycle

Many leases require the statement within 90 to 120 days after the lease year ends. That window is lease-specific. Some forms say a late statement waives the right to collect a balance. Others say deliver it when you can. Calendar each deadline as if it were the strict kind. Finding out which kind you have in March is how deadlines get missed.

| Stage | Work | Illustrative timing | Output |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Compile | A year of expenses, coded in or out per lease | January–February | Ledger by category |
| 2. Share | Pro-rata math, anchor exclusions, the right denominator | February | Share schedule |
| 3. Cap | Apply each cap; separate pass-through items | February–March | Capped obligation |
| 4. Statement | Actual versus estimates; balance or credit | March | One statement per tenant |
| 5. Collect | Bill, credit, and answer questions | March–April | Cash or a credit on the ledger |

The statement itself is short: the year, total includable cost, the tenant's percentage, the tenant's dollars, estimates paid, and the settlement. Keep the ledger, the invoices, and the share math where you can send them. A tenant who can see the arithmetic argues about a line. A tenant who cannot see it argues about you.

**Illustrative settlement.** Same 2,400-square-foot tenant, 3.20% of a $180,000 CAM pool, with no cap in play: $5,760. They paid $450 a month, $5,400 for the year. The statement bills $360. If they had paid $520 a month, the statement carries a $480 credit. Same lease, opposite direction. Show both numbers.

## Audits and disputes

Most triple-net forms allow an audit for a window after the statement, often 12 to 24 months, on written notice, sometimes only if the tenant is current, sometimes only by a defined kind of accountant. After the window, many leases treat the statement as final. That is a clause to abstract, not a rule to assume.

Audits follow large true-ups, a sharp year-over-year jump, or a belief that capital or a management fee landed in the pool. The defense is the binder you built in stage 1: ledger, invoices, the lease sentence for the share, the cap math with last year's actual attached, and the statement. Send that. Most line-item fights end when the paper is already organized. An illustrative map:

| Finding | Tenant's point | Useful response |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Capital in the pool | Exclude it, or amortize it | Scope documents and the lease definition |
| Fee over the cap | Credit the excess | Recalculate and credit if they are right |
| Wrong share | Anchor should be out of the denominator | The lease sentence; recalculate if you missed it |
| Category not listed | Remove it | The includable list; remove it if it is not there |
| Duplicate | Billed twice | The ledger; correct a real duplicate |

A tenant who says "this is wrong" without a line item has not given you a dispute you can fix. Ask, in writing, which lines. Pay or credit what is actually wrong. A vague objection is not, by itself, a reason to pause the whole bill — unless that tenant's lease says otherwise. Read the dispute clause before you threaten or before you concede.

## A draft the operator still signs

Reconciliation is repetitive math on top of judgment calls. Software can sort a ledger export, line up lease fields you have already checked, flag an expense that might be capital, and compute shares and caps. A person verifies the lease fields against the document, decides the ambiguous lines, spot-checks at least two tenants by hand, and approves every statement before it is sent. An incorrect statement that goes out is a billing dispute with your name on it. The review is the control. Speed without that review is how errors scale.

Do not describe a model as having "done" the reconciliation. It prepared a draft.

## Planning ranges, labeled as such

These are illustrative ranges operators use to see whether a draft statement is in a familiar band. They are not a survey, and a deal outside them is not automatically wrong.

| Figure | Illustrative band | Why it moves |
| --- | --- | --- |
| All-in CAM | $3–$8 per square foot per year | Age, market, amenities |
| Open-air strip | $2–$5 | Simpler common area |
| Enclosed mall | $8–$20 | HVAC, security, interior common area |
| Management fee | 3–6% of collections | Often capped in the lease |
| Admin add-on | 10–15% of direct CAM | Some leases exclude it |
| Statement deadline | 90–120 days | The lease |
| Audit window | 12–24 months | The lease |
| Controllable cap | 3–5% a year | Taxes, insurance, utilities often outside |
| True-up versus annual CAM | 5–15% | A larger gap means the estimate was lazy |

A true-up that keeps landing at the top of that last band is an estimating problem. Fix next year's monthly number. Do not wait for another surprise.

## Questions that come up at year-end

**We missed the deadline.** Read that lease. Some waive the balance. Some require delivery as soon as you can. Either way, send a correct statement and do not miss the next one.

**They will not pay while they audit.** Many leases require payment, then a refund if the audit wins. Some allow a withhold of the disputed slice only. Follow the clause.

**They object and will not say why.** Ask for the lines in writing. Answer those lines with the binder.

**Cap versus stop.** A cap limits the increase. A stop is a threshold the landlord absorbs. Different clauses.

**Occupied area or gross leasable area?** Gross leasable area keeps the share stable when space is empty. Occupied area pushes vacancy onto the tenants who remain. New leases should say which one, on purpose.

**A capital job helps the whole center.** If the lease allows amortization, bill the annual slice, not the check you wrote the contractor. Keep the schedule in the binder.

## The practical next step

1. Abstract every lease's pool, denominator, anchor exclusion, cap type, deadline, and audit window onto one schedule.
2. Code this year's expenses against that schedule before you compute a single share.
3. Build the statement so the arithmetic fits on one page, with invoices behind it.
4. Calendar each deadline as a hard date.
5. Let software draft. Do not let it send.
