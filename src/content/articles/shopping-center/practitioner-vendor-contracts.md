# Vendor Management for Shopping Centers: Contracts, Performance, and Termination

A shopping center owner does not maintain the property so much as manage the people who do. The shorter series piece, "Vendor Management: Roofs, Lots, Landscaping, and HVAC," covers the bench, the one-page scope, and the certificate file. This practitioner reference goes further: how to bid, what a contract has to say about insurance and indemnity, which numbers you review and how often, how security and HVAC contracts differ from landscaping, and how to end a relationship without a gap in coverage.

Insurance limits, notice periods, and indemnity language below are illustrative terms operators write into forms. They are not a legal standard, and they are not a claim about any real property or vendor. Whether a clause is enforceable, and whether a limit is enough, is counsel's and the insurer's work. The operator's work is to have the clause, the certificate, and a person who reads both.

## Five kinds of vendors

Group the bench before you write one agreement. The categories overlap, and the contracts should not.

- **Maintenance and repair.** HVAC, plumbing, electrical, roofing, and a general handyman for everything else.
- **Landscaping.** Mowing, edging, irrigation, seasonal color, and tree care.
- **Cleaning.** Common-area janitorial, waste, and pressure washing.
- **Security.** Guards or patrol, alarm monitoring, and camera maintenance, if you use them.
- **Specialized.** Fire protection, elevators where you have them, pest control, and signage.

Each category needs its own scope, its own performance measures, and its own way out. A single "property services" paragraph covering all five is how disputes start.

In a hot climate, the HVAC contractor is often the vendor tenants feel first. A failed unit does not cost you a tenant because of the invoice. It costs you the tenant's customers, who stop coming. Write an emergency response time into that contract before the first hot weekend, and keep a contractor who will commit to it. Four hours is a common planning target for an emergency call. It is a contract term you negotiate, not a regulation.

## Bid when the dollars repeat

Any recurring contract above a threshold you set — an illustrative line is $5,000 a year — should be bid. The bid forces a written scope, gives you a price record, and makes "we have always used them" a choice instead of a habit. Three qualified bidders is a workable minimum. For a larger award, an illustrative line is $25,000 a year, five bids is a better record.

Pre-qualify before you invite the bid. A low price from a vendor who cannot finish the work or carry insurance is not a bargain. A practical screen:

- Long enough in business to have finished comparable jobs (three years is a common floor)
- References from properties of similar size and use
- The ability to carry the insurance the contract will require
- A safety record you can ask about, including workers-compensation experience where the trade has one
- Licenses the trade actually requires

A vendor who fails the screen does not get a bid package, including when the number looks attractive.

Weight the decision in writing so price cannot silently become the only factor. An illustrative scorecard:

| Criterion | Minimum standard | Illustrative weight |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Price | In range of the other qualified bids | 30% |
| Similar properties | Three or more years at a comparable size | 25% |
| Insurance | Meets the limits in the invitation | 20% |
| References | Three you can call | 15% |
| Response time | Emergency 4 hours; routine 24–48 hours | 10% |

Change the weights. Do not skip them. Document who you invited, who declined, and why the winner won.

## Scope, insurance, and indemnity

The contract does three jobs. It defines the work tightly enough to score. It requires insurance that responds if the vendor causes a loss. It allocates the vendor's negligence to the vendor. Ambiguity in the scope is the usual fight. "Landscaping maintenance" means one thing to you and another to the crew. "Mow all turf weekly from March through October, edge hard surfaces every other week, and keep shrubs at or below 36 inches" can be photographed and scored.

**Insurance, as a form operators often attach.** Commercial general liability on the order of $1 million per occurrence and $2 million aggregate, workers' compensation at the statutory limit for the state where the work happens, and auto liability if vehicles operate on the property. Name the owner and the manager as additional insureds on the general liability policy where the form allows it. Collect a certificate at signing, at each renewal, and when you ask. A certificate is evidence of coverage at a moment in time. It is not the policy. If a claim matters, the policy is what counsel and the insurer will read.

**Indemnity.** A common landlord form requires the vendor to indemnify, defend, and hold the owner harmless for claims arising out of the work, to the extent caused by the vendor's negligence or willful misconduct. Mutual indemnity, where you also indemnify the vendor for your own operations, is a different deal and usually the wrong default on a service contract. Have counsel read the clause you actually sign. Do not assume a template survived contact with the vendor's redline.

Emergency rates belong in the contract before the emergency. So does a rule that work above a stated dollar amount needs written authorization first. If a vendor does weekend work and invoices a rate nobody agreed, you will argue about a "reasonable" number after the fact. That argument is avoidable.

## What you measure, and how often

A contract without a review is a check. Put the measures in the agreement: the metric, the cadence, and what happens when the number misses. An illustrative set:

| Category | What to count | Review |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Landscaping | Appearance score, visits completed, complaints | Monthly |
| HVAC and mechanical | Emergency response time, preventive visits completed, repeat failures | Quarterly |
| Security | Incident response time, patrols completed, report quality | Monthly |
| Janitorial | Cleanliness score, complaints, supplies restocked | Weekly |
| Parking lot | Defects open, sweeps completed, lighting outages | Monthly |

Appearance and cleanliness scores only work if two people would score the same photograph the same way. Define the scale in the scope. Keep the complaints in one log, not in a text thread.

## Lots and landscaping are what customers see

The lot and the landscaping are the arrival. They are also where slip-and-fall claims and overpayment tend to live. Specify mowing height and frequency, edging, shrub limits, irrigation checks, seasonal color if you pay for it, and debris removal. For the lot, specify sweep frequency, crack repair, pothole response time, restripe timing, and how fast a dark pole gets a lamp.

Tenants pay for the lot through recoveries, and they notice it. A lot that is swept, patched, and readable signals that someone is minding the property. A lot with holes and faded lines signals the opposite. The cost gap between those two standards is often smaller than the argument about it. Write the standard, then inspect it on the cadence above.

## Security contracts carry a different risk

A missed patrol or a guard's action can become the owner's problem in a way a missed mowing usually does not. The scope should name routes, how often they are walked, what the guard does for each kind of incident, what gets written down, and who gets called. Ask for professional liability in addition to general liability and workers' compensation. The indemnity should speak to failure to respond and to the acts of individual guards. Counsel drafts that sentence. You do not leave it as "vendor shall be responsible."

Incident reports are part of the work product. If they are late, thin, or missing, that is a performance miss, not a paperwork quibble.

## HVAC: preventive work is the contract

HVAC is often the largest maintenance category, and the one where delay costs the most in tenant disruption. A preventive contract specifies quarterly inspections of rooftop units, coil cleaning on a set schedule, filter changes, and a written record of every visit. It also specifies the emergency response time and a different rate for emergency calls than for scheduled ones. Reactive-only service feels cheaper until the first weekend failure. The preventive schedule is how you stay out of that weekend.

## Ending the contract without a hole

Every agreement needs a way out for convenience and a way out for cause. Illustrative mechanics operators negotiate:

| Type | Notice | Cure | Transition |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Convenience | 30 days, written | None | Name the replacement before you send notice |
| Cause, performance | Written default | 10–15 days | Start the replacement search the day you send notice |
| Cause, insurance lapse | Written, immediate | 5 days to reinstate | Stop work until coverage is back |
| Mutual | A written agreement | None | Ask the departing vendor for a handover |

The dangerous days are the days between notice and the new vendor's first visit. For HVAC and security, a gap is not acceptable. Identify the replacement, confirm a start date, and only then send the notice. If insurance lapses, stop the work and say so in writing. A vendor with no coverage is not a vendor you can keep "until we sort it out." One incident in that window is the scenario the certificate requirement exists to prevent. Whether the owner is liable anyway is counsel's question. The operating rule is: no coverage, no work.

If the contract has no convenience termination, you may still have a cause path, and you have to follow its notice and cure steps. Read them before you announce a decision in a phone call.

## Where software helps, and where it must not decide

A useful vendor file tracks the contract term, the insurance requirements, the certificate expiration, the performance log, and the renewal date. Alerts belong on the calendar: a certificate 60 days before it expires, a renewal review 90 days before the term ends. Software can sort that file, draft a reminder, and flag a missed visit. A person selects the vendor, scores the work, and decides to terminate. Those decisions depend on relationships, site conditions, and risk tolerance that a model does not carry. A dashboard that looks tidy is not a decision.

## Questions operators ask

**How many bids?** Three above your recurring threshold. More when the award is large enough that a thin record would bother you, or a lender, later.

**What insurance?** The limits in the section above are a common form, not a minimum the law hands you. Security vendors usually need professional liability as well. Confirm additional-insured status on the certificate you received, not on the one you requested.

**Can I end it mid-term?** If the contract says you can, on the notice it states. If it does not, cause still requires the default and cure path the document describes.

**The certificate expired.** Stop the work. Written notice. A short window to reinstate, then termination if they do not. Do not hope the policy is still in force.

**They billed an emergency rate that is not in the contract.** Pay what you can justify, in writing, and put the rate in the next agreement. Require written approval before non-emergency work over your threshold.

## The practical next step

1. List every recurring vendor, the annual dollars, the expiration, and the certificate date on one page.
2. Rewrite the two scopes tenants see first — landscaping and the lot — until a photograph can prove the work.
3. Put emergency response time and emergency rates in the HVAC agreement before summer.
4. Bid the next renewal that sits above your threshold. Keep the scorecard.
5. Name a replacement before you terminate anyone who covers HVAC or security.
