# Shopping Center Acquisition Due Diligence: The Operator's Complete Checklist

A signed purchase agreement starts a short clock. The work inside that clock is not a tour. It is a test of whether the income you underwrote is the income the leases, the building, and the land will actually produce. The shorter series piece, "The Retail Due Diligence Checklist: Leases, Estoppels, Title, and Physical Condition," is the phase overview. This practitioner reference is the working checklist behind it: the fifteen lease terms worth abstracting, how to read tenant health without a live rent roll, what a physical and environmental file has to contain, how to normalize a seller's NOI, and how to close without leaving money or paper behind.

Nothing here is legal, tax, environmental, or investment advice. Thresholds, costs, and timelines below are illustrative planning figures for a hypothetical open-air center. They are not quotes, ordinances, or a claim about any real property. Counsel, the environmental consultant, and the surveyor own their conclusions. The operator owns the open-items list.

Due diligence is also the first draft of the operating plan. Every abstract, every deferred-maintenance line, and every tenant conversation is a note about what you will do after closing. Treat the file that way.

## Why a shopping center file is different

Office and industrial diligence can often stop at credit, term, and a building report. An open-air center adds a web of relationships. A dark anchor can trip co-tenancy language in inline leases and change rent, termination rights, or both. National, regional, and local tenants do not carry the same guaranty, the same sales reporting, or the same recovery structure. Common-area charges are a second set of contracts sitting inside the first, and they are a frequent source of disputes.

Read past the offering memorandum. Ask whether the mix still fits the trade area, and whether a vacancy in one box changes the economics of the others. Those questions belong in the model before they belong in a surprise after closing.

## The fifteen lease terms worth abstracting

The lease is the income. Abstract every executed lease, amendment, assignment, guaranty, and side letter. A rent roll is the seller's summary. The lease is what you can enforce. For each document, capture these fifteen items, then write what each one does to cash flow and to operating flexibility. An abstract that only copies clauses is a filing task. The analysis is the work.

1. **Rent.** Base rent, percentage rent if any (rate and breakpoint), and scheduled steps.
2. **Term and options.** Original term, renewal options, and termination rights, including kick-outs and co-tenancy.
3. **CAM and operating expenses.** Inclusions, exclusions, caps, administrative fees, and audit rights.
4. **Taxes.** Pro-rata share, and whether the lease uses a base year or a straight share of the bill.
5. **Insurance.** What the tenant must carry, and any waiver of subrogation.
6. **Use.** Permitted use, exclusive use, and prohibited uses.
7. **Assignment and subletting.** Consent standard, and any landlord recapture right.
8. **Repair and maintenance.** Who holds the roof, structure, and HVAC.
9. **Default and remedies.** Notice and cure periods, as the document states them.
10. **Tenant improvements.** Unfunded allowance and any future landlord obligation.
11. **Guaranties.** Personal or corporate, and any burn-off.
12. **Co-tenancy.** The trigger, and the remedy (reduced rent, termination, or both).
13. **Expansion or contraction.** Rights that change the square footage later.
14. **Signage.** What is permitted, including pylon or monument rights.
15. **Estoppel and SNDA.** Whether the tenant's certificate and any subordination agreement match the lease you just read.

Plan on a few hours per lease for a careful abstract. A legacy center with leases from several eras will not share one CAM definition. Build the abstract lease by lease.

## Tenant health without borrowing a live roll

A center is only as durable as the businesses in it. Ask for sales history where the lease already requires the tenant to report it. Look for trend and for sales per square foot, and label anything you do not have as unknown. Visitation studies can describe a trade area; they are an input, not a substitute for the lease file. For a national tenant, corporate credit is public information you can read. For a local tenant, ask for financials the purchase agreement lets you request, and do not invent a credit score you were not given.

Confirm that a guaranty exists and that the guarantor is the party named. A quick look at public reviews tells you how the store treats customers. It does not tell you whether the rent is paid. Pair the roll with a delinquency report. A tenant who looks current on base rent and is ninety days behind on recoveries is a credit issue the roll will hide.

## Physical condition and a capital model

Walk the property, then pay people who inspect this building type for a living. The list is ordinary and still where buyers get surprised:

- Roof: age, condition, warranty, and leak history
- HVAC: age of units, service records, and a replacement horizon
- Parking lot: paving, striping, drainage, and lighting
- Facade: paint, masonry, and anything deferred
- Common areas: landscaping, walks, dumpster enclosures, monument signs
- Utilities: electrical, plumbing, and sewer condition as the inspector finds them

Ask for near-term repairs and a ten-year capital sketch with costs attached. An illustrative planning range operators use before that report arrives is on the order of $0.50 to $1.50 per square foot per year in reserves. It is a placeholder, not a bid. Replace it with the property condition assessment. The seller's books often show a reserve of zero. Yours should not.

## Environmental review

A Phase I environmental site assessment is the standard first look: records, a site visit, and interviews, aimed at recognized environmental conditions. Former dry cleaning, fuel, auto repair, or manufacturing on the site or next door is a reason to pay attention, not a conclusion. If the Phase I identifies a condition that needs testing, a Phase II (soil or groundwater sampling) is the usual next step. Order it when the report says the condition is there. Do not order it on every deal to feel thorough, and do not skip it to save the fee when the consultant has named a condition.

On older buildings, ask about asbestos and lead paint as building materials, and about their condition. Hire an environmental consultant who does this work. An illustrative Phase I fee in the range of $2,500 to $4,500 is a planning number, not a proposal. The cost of a cleanup you did not price is the number that changes the deal.

## Title, survey, and parking

Read the title commitment, not just the policy jacket. Reciprocal easement agreements, declarations, and covenants control shared access, shared parking, and shared maintenance with neighbors. They survive the purchase. A survey should confirm lines, easements, setbacks, and where the improvements actually sit. Reconcile the parking count on the survey to the count in the zoning approval. Many ordinances are discussed around four spaces per 1,000 square feet. The number that governs this property is the one on its approval. A mismatch can constrain leasing or financing. Say so in the open-items list while the seller still has a reason to fix it.

Confirm that current zoning allows the uses on the rent roll and any use you underwrote as vacant-space lease-up. Restrictions and development limits belong in the model, not in a footnote after closing.

## Normalizing the seller's NOI

The seller's trailing income is a starting point. Normalize it to the operations you will actually run. The table is **illustrative only**. It is not a real center.

| Category | Seller's books | Operator's adjustment | Normalized |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Gross potential rent | $1,000,000 | Mark rents that will not repeat | $950,000 |
| Vacancy | 5% | Use a supportable loss factor | 8% |
| CAM expenses | $100,000 | Add items the seller omitted | $120,000 |
| Management fee | 3% of EGI | Use the fee you will actually pay | 4% of EGI |
| Capital reserves | $0 | Fund a reserve | $0.75 per square foot |

Adjust for market vacancy, for operating costs the seller has been understating, for a management fee you will pay, and for a capital reserve. Free rent and unfunded tenant-improvement obligations hit cash in specific months. Put them on the timeline. When you take a number off the seller's net operating income, write the reason next to it. A buyer who can show the math can have the price conversation. A buyer who only says the number feels high cannot.

## Closing conditions and adjustments

The purchase agreement lists what must be true before you fund. Track each contingency: financing, environmental, title, physical condition, and delivery of tenant estoppels. Estoppels are the tenant's version of the lease facts: rent, expiration, options, deposit, and whether the landlord owes anything. When the estoppel and the lease file disagree, you want that disagreement before closing. A key tenant who refuses to sign is a flag. Depending on the contract, it can be a reason to walk or a reason to hold money back. That reading is counsel's. Your job is to notice the refusal early.

Prorate rent, recoveries, taxes, and utilities to the closing date and check the arithmetic. Know which holdbacks exist for repairs, unfinished tenant work, or open disputes, and who releases them.

## A six-week clock

Thirty to sixty days is a common diligence window. It is a planning range, not a statute. A six-week shape that keeps third parties from slipping:

- **Week 1.** Kickoff, document request (rent roll, leases, financials, survey, prior environmental), and engagement of the inspector and environmental consultant.
- **Weeks 2–3.** Abstracts and a first financial pass, in parallel with the site visit.
- **Week 4.** Reports in hand, a first title read, and tenant conversations the contract allows.
- **Week 5.** Issues list, and a price or repair conversation if the file changed the income or the capital.
- **Week 6.** Estoppels, final reports, and a closing checklist.

One open-items list, with a name and a date on every line, is the whole management system. Re-run the model at the end. If rent is lower, expenses are higher, or capital is nearer than the memorandum said, the price conversation happens before closing.

## Questions that come up in the file

**What gets missed most often?** Co-tenancy, and tenant financials nobody asked for. Either one can change the income stream you thought you bought.

**How much do estoppels matter?** They are the tenant's statement of the deal. Lenders often require them from major tenants. Start early. Response windows in leases are often measured in weeks, not days.

**Do I always need a Phase II?** When the Phase I identifies a recognized environmental condition that calls for sampling. Not as a default on a clean Phase I.

**What reserve is reasonable?** Age and condition decide it. Use $0.50 to $1.50 per square foot per year only until the property condition assessment replaces it.

**The seller's NOI looks full.** Normalize line by line, show the adjustments, and negotiate from that page.

**A key tenant will not estoppel.** Treat it as an open item with a dollar consequence, and let counsel read the contract's remedy. Do not close around it in silence.

## The practical next step

1. Open one list on day one: every missing document, every discrepancy, every report not yet ordered, with an owner and a date.
2. Abstract the fifteen terms for every lease before you argue about price.
3. Order environmental and physical work immediately. Their calendars do not care about yours.
4. Normalize NOI on a single page and attach the reason for each adjustment.
5. Re-trade or walk before the contingency dies, not after.
