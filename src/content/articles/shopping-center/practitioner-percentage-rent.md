# Percentage Rent Clauses: How to Calculate, Audit, and Negotiate Them

Percentage rent, also called overage, is additional rent equal to a rate times the tenant's gross sales above a breakpoint. It showed up in department-store deals when landlords took a lower base rent in exchange for a share of sales. It still shows up in inline leases, especially food, entertainment, and other high-volume uses. When it is drafted carefully, the landlord participates in a good year and the tenant's fixed rent is lower than it would be in a pure base-rent deal. When it is drafted loosely, the landlord has a clause and no reliable number.

The shorter series piece, "The Anatomy of a Retail Lease: NNN, Gross, and Modified Gross," names percentage rent as one moving part. This practitioner reference is the calculation, the definition of sales, the audit, and the underwriting. It is context, not counsel. What "gross sales" includes, and what an audit may demand, is what the lease says. Enforcing it is counsel's work when a tenant disagrees.

Figures are illustrative. Where a tenant is named, it is **Lantern Cafe** at the fictional Cedar Crossing center from the rent-roll practitioner reference: 2,400 square feet, not a real shop.

## The formula

Percentage rent = (gross sales − breakpoint) × rate, and zero when sales do not clear the breakpoint.

Three jobs, from the owner's side. It is upside in a strong year. It is a correction when base rent was set low. And a tenant who pays it, year after year, is reporting sales strong enough to matter at renewal. That last point is information. It is not, by itself, a reason to assume the sales were complete.

## Natural and artificial breakpoints

A natural breakpoint is annual base rent divided by the rate. At that sales level, the overage on sales above the line is zero, and the base rent equals the rate times sales. Neither side has tilted the threshold yet.

**Lantern Cafe, illustrative.**

- Area: 2,400 square feet
- Base rent: $35 per square foot, $84,000 a year
- Rate: 6%
- Natural breakpoint: $84,000 ÷ 0.06 = $1,400,000
- If sales are $1,400,000, percentage rent is $0
- If sales are $1,800,000, overage is $400,000, and percentage rent is $400,000 × 0.06 = $24,000
- Total rent: $84,000 + $24,000 = $108,000, or $45 per square foot

An artificial breakpoint is a negotiated number above or below that line. Higher favors the tenant: sales must go further before overage starts. Lower favors the landlord. A fixed dollar breakpoint in an older lease is artificial by another name. Anchors often push the breakpoint up, because their sales per foot would trip a natural breakpoint quickly if the base rent is low.

| Breakpoint | How it is set | Tends to favor | Where you see it |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Natural | Base rent ÷ rate | Neither, at the math | Ordinary inline forms |
| Artificial, high | Negotiated above natural | Tenant | High-volume and anchor deals |
| Artificial, low | Negotiated below natural | Landlord | When you have the leverage to ask |
| Fixed dollars | A stated sales number | Whoever guessed the volume | Older leases |

If you cannot compute the natural breakpoint from the LOI, the rate or the base rent is not finished.

## Gross sales is the economic term

The definition of gross sales moves the result more than the rate does. Treat it as economics.

A landlord-leaning definition includes revenue from the premises however it is paid: cash, card, layaway, gift cards at redemption rather than at sale, service charges, delivery charges, and orders placed from, filled from, or attributed to the store. Tenant drafts cut the other way. An illustrative negotiation list:

| Proposed exclusion | Tenant's point | A common landlord response |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sales tax collected | It is not revenue | Accept it |
| Returns and refunds | They are not net sales | Accept them, against documents |
| Employee discounts | Not a market sale | Cap the exclusion |
| Internet orders | Not from the store | Include them if the store fills them |
| Gift cards at issuance | The sale is not finished | Include them at redemption |
| Wholesale | A different channel | Exclude only a truly separate business |
| Catering off site | Not from the premises | Negotiate; brand-attributed events are the fight |

Buy-online-pickup-in-store, curbside, and ship-from-store sit in the gap. Older leases often never mention them. Tenants then argue that an order filled from the suite is not a sale "in" the suite. Any new lease or renewal should say whether those orders count. Do not leave it to a preamble about "all sales on the premises" and hope.

## Reporting and the audit

Percentage rent without a report is a hope. A workable clause asks for:

- A monthly sales report, due a stated number of days after month-end
- An annual statement, due a stated number of days after year-end, certified by a named role (an officer, or an accountant — say which)
- Notice if the tenant restates the numbers

Audit rights should say how far back you may look, that you may see the books the clause names (summaries only, or the point-of-sale record), who pays if the audit finds a shortfall above a threshold, and how fast a shortfall must be paid. A common planning shape is a multi-year lookback and a cost shift if underreporting clears something like 3 to 5 percent. Without a cost shift, the worst case for a short report is paying what was owed. That is a weak incentive.

| Term | Landlord draft | Tenant draft |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Lookback | Three years | One year |
| Certificate | An accountant | An officer only |
| Audit cost | Tenant pays above a shortfall threshold | Each side pays itself |
| How often | Yearly | Once every several years |
| Records | Point-of-sale access | Summary reports |
| Cure | A short window after the finding | A long window and a dispute right |

These are drafting poles. Where a deal lands is negotiation. Write the landing spot down. "Audit rights" with no window and no records list is not a right you can schedule.

## Annual versus monthly

Most clauses compute once a year. The tenant reports the year, you compute the overage, and payment follows the annual statement by a stated number of days. It is simple. It also means the first month of the lease year may not be collected until the following year.

A monthly payment against a monthly breakpoint, with an annual true-up, moves cash forward and adds administrative work. It shows up more often when the dollars are large. Pick one, and build the calendar to match. Do not run a monthly process against an annual clause, or the reverse, because a spreadsheet made it convenient.

## How to negotiate without their sales history

The tenant knows the volume. You often do not. Close some of that gap before you trade the breakpoint. Public companies publish comparable-store figures. Category ranges from brokers are a starting conversation, not a fact about this tenant. If you have no sales, do not invent a breakpoint that "feels" like their concept. Ask for history the lease process allows, or price the base rent as if overage may never arrive.

| Lever | Landlord aim | Tenant aim | A place deals often pause |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Breakpoint | Natural, or below | Well above natural | Modestly above natural |
| Rate | Higher on inline uses | Lower | Depends on the category |
| Sales definition | Broad, including store-fulfilled orders | Exclude online and services | Store-fulfilled in, pure online out |
| Audit | Annual, longer lookback, cost shift | Rare, short lookback, each side pays | A middle window and a cost shift at a stated percent |
| Reports | Monthly, plus a certified annual | Annual, internal | Quarterly, plus a certified annual |

Anchor economics and inline economics are different deals. An anchor at a low base rent and a high volume will trip a natural breakpoint easily, so the negotiation moves to a higher breakpoint, exclusions (fuel, pharmacy, online), and a narrower audit. An inline tenant at a higher base rent may never reach the breakpoint. Overage is then a renewal signal, not a budget line. Illustrative planning bands, not a survey:

| | Grocer-style anchor | Drug-style anchor | Food inline | Service inline |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Base rent | Lower per foot | Mid | Higher | Mid to higher |
| Rate | About 1–1.5% | About 2–3% | About 6–8% | About 5–7% |
| When overage appears | If the breakpoint stayed low | Sometimes | When the shop is busy | Sometimes |

Do not paste these rates into a term sheet because the table looked authoritative. They are a way to notice when a proposal is far from a familiar shape.

## Underwriting: overage is not base rent

Lenders often size debt on base rent and recoveries, and leave percentage rent out, or they take a haircut of a documented average. Ask before you put $24,000 of Lantern Cafe overage into a debt-service model.

For an acquisition, a conservative approach is a multi-year average of overage actually received, then a further haircut, with no growth unless you have sales evidence. In a value-add story — low base rents, strong reported sales — model each paying tenant, then cut sales by a stress you can explain (an illustrative 15 to 20 percent) and keep only the overage that survives. The survivor can be in the case you will defend. The rest is upside.

If percentage rent is a large share of a tenant's total rent, the income moves with sales. An illustrative flag is overage above half of that tenant's total rent. Base rent should still carry the operation.

## An audit workflow a person finishes

1. **Abstract the clause.** Rate, breakpoint and whether it is natural, the sales definition, report dates, lookback, cost shift, cure. Flag odd exclusions and weak audit language for a human read. A model can extract. It can also skip a side letter.
2. **Recompute.** From the reports the tenant actually sent, calculate what the clause says was due, and compare it to what was paid. Variances get a line, not a vibe.
3. **Compare to a benchmark you can name.** A food tenant reporting a very low sales-per-foot figure against a category range you sourced is a reason to look, not proof of a short report.
4. **Read omnichannel against the definition.** If the lease includes store-fulfilled orders and the report has none, that is a question for the tenant and, if they stonewall, for counsel. It is not a number to bill because a model guessed their website volume.

No statement goes to a tenant, and no underwriting memo treats overage as fact, until a person has checked the clause and the arithmetic.

## Flags

| Item | A calmer draft | A reason to renegotiate or look |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Inline rate | In a familiar mid-single to high-single digit band | A token rate with no other concession you wanted |
| Anchor rate | About 1–2% if you kept a real breakpoint | A fraction of a percent and a breakpoint you will never hit |
| Breakpoint | Near natural | Far above natural, with no base-rent trade you can see |
| Lookback | Long enough to cover a late discovery | A window that closes before you can staff an audit |
| Cost shift | Tenant pays if the shortfall clears a stated percent | No shift |
| Online orders | Store-fulfilled orders are in the definition | Silence |
| Reports | Periodic, plus a certified annual | Once a year, unsigned |
| Overage vs. base | Base rent still carries the suite | The deal only works if sales stay high |

## The practical next step

1. Compute the natural breakpoint on every percentage-rent lease you already have. Note which ones are artificial, and by how much.
2. Read the gross-sales definition for store-fulfilled orders. List the leases that are silent.
3. Put report dates and audit windows on the same calendar as CAM deadlines.
4. Recompute last year's overage from the reports. Variances become an open-items list.
5. Keep overage out of the base-case debt model until a lender says otherwise.
