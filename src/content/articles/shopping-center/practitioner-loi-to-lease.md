# LOI to Lease: The Shopping Center Leasing Process from LOI to Execution

A letter of intent is not a lease. Landlords and tenants still behave as if the interesting decisions are over once the LOI is signed. They are not. The shorter series piece, "Finding and Negotiating with Retail Tenants," covers the pipeline and the tradeoffs. This practitioner reference stays on the path after a prospect is real: what the LOI should lock, what it must not lock, how a deal moves from draft to signature, where tenant-improvement money gets into trouble, and which failures show up after everyone thinks the deal is done.

This is context, not counsel. Whether an LOI is enforceable, whether a lien reaches the property, and whether a lender must approve the lease are legal questions. The operating rules below are how an owner keeps those questions from arriving as surprises. Dollar ranges are illustrative planning figures for a hypothetical inline deal, not a market survey.

## What the LOI commits you to

The LOI is a framework. It records the economics both sides will draft from, and it should say, in words a non-lawyer can find, that it is not itself the lease. A sentence operators put on the page: this letter is not intended to create a binding agreement, and either party may stop negotiations before a fully negotiated lease is signed. Have counsel read that sentence in the draft you are about to send. A shift from "the parties intend" to "the parties agree" is the kind of edit counsel is there to catch. You do not need a theory of what a court would do. You need the draft to say what you mean, and you need counsel to confirm it says that.

Do not put a finished obligation in the LOI by accident. "Landlord will provide a $40,000 tenant-improvement allowance" can be read later as a promise made before anyone reviewed the lease form. If the allowance is still a proposal, write it as a proposal.

Lock the economic skeleton, and leave the machinery to the lease:

- Base rent and the escalation
- Term and renewal options, including how renewal rent is set
- Tenant-improvement allowance, how it is paid, and what happens to unused dollars
- Free rent, and what it is tied to
- Permitted use, and any exclusivity
- Delivery date, in plain language

CAM structure, insurance, assignment, and default belong in the lease, where they can be drafted against your form. If a point will change the model, it still belongs in the LOI even if it is "legal." An exclusivity and a co-tenancy trigger are economics. Do not discover them in the third redline.

## Seven stages, and where deals stall

A clean inline deal often runs 90 to 120 days from first serious contact to rent commencement. That is an illustrative duration, not a rule. Deals that run long usually stall while the lease is being drafted or while the LOI economics were never actually finished.

| Stage | Work | Illustrative duration | Decision |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Qualify | Credit, use, and fit with existing exclusives | 3–7 days | Pursue or decline |
| 2. Proposal | Rent, term, allowance, a plan of the space | 5–10 days | The walk-away number |
| 3. LOI | Economics, exclusivity, delivery | 10–21 days | Final LOI terms |
| 4. Draft | Your form, their redlines | 21–45 days | Which concessions you will sign |
| 5. Execute | Signatures, deposit, insurance | 5–10 days | Conditions met, or not |
| 6. Build-out | Delivery, allowance draws, construction | 30–120 days | Spend and schedule |
| 7. Rent start | Opening, rent, recovery proration | The commencement date | The date in the lease, confirmed |

Start on your lease form. The tenant's form is written to their risk. You can accept changes. You should not start from their stack and try to find your way home.

## Economics the LOI has to settle

Five variables, if left open, get filled later by whoever has more leverage. That is often the tenant's counsel, working against a clock you created.

**Base rent and bumps.** State rent as annual dollars per square foot, not only as a monthly check. Name the bump: a fixed percent, a fixed dollar step, or an index. Index bumps feel fair and are harder to underwrite. Fixed steps are boring and readable. Boring is a virtue in a rent roll.

**Term and options.** An illustrative inline shape is five years with one or two five-year options. Anchors often want a longer initial term and rights on adjacent space. Write the renewal rent in the LOI. "At market" without a definition is a future argument. Define the method, or set a step off the expiring rent.

**Allowance.** Express it per square foot. Say when it is paid (invoices, or milestones), and whether unused money becomes free rent or disappears. Do not design the build-out in the LOI. Do attach a one-page description of what the allowance is meant to cover, so "we thought it included the hood" is not a surprise. The work letter on the lease carries the real scope.

**Illustrative allowance.** A 2,000-square-foot inline tenant at $40 per square foot is an $80,000 check before the first rent dollar. Restaurant uses often need more because of plumbing and exhaust. A planning band operators use for ordinary inline retail is on the order of $25 to $65 per square foot, and higher for food. It is not a quote. Match the allowance to the term. A ten-year lease can carry a larger allowance than a five-year lease because you have longer to earn it back. That is underwriting, not a rule.

## Where the lease actually moves

Each clause has a landlord pole, a tenant pole, and a place deals often land. Know your pole before the call. An illustrative map, not a statement of "market":

| Provision | Landlord draft | Tenant draft | A frequent landing spot |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Assignment | Consent required, no recapture | Free assignment | Consent not unreasonably withheld, plus a recapture right |
| Exclusive use | None, or a narrow use | A wide category | A named use, narrowly written |
| CAM audit | None | Open-ended, years back | Once a year, a defined window |
| Guaranty | Full, no burn-off | None | Burns off after a period of on-time payment |
| Force majeure | Narrow, rent still due | Broad, rent abated | Time extended, rent not excused |
| Holdover | A high multiple, then month to month | No premium | A stated multiple, and a right to terminate |

Exclusives already in the center are a map you draw before the LOI, not during lease drafting. A use that collides with an existing exclusive is a deal killer you can see on day one.

Recovery structure belongs in the LOI if it is not your standard triple-net form. In triple net, the tenant pays a share of taxes, insurance, and CAM, and your residual exposure is structural and capital work, subject to whatever amortization the lease allows. A cap on controllable CAM — an illustrative 3 to 5 percent a year, with taxes, insurance, and utilities outside it — changes the ten-year model. If you agree to it, model it. The companion practitioner piece, "CAM Reconciliation for Shopping Centers: The Landlord's Complete Guide," is the annual machinery. Do not negotiate the cap as if the bill will run itself.

## Tenant improvements and the work letter

The allowance is often the largest check you write on a new deal. The work letter, an exhibit, should state:

- Landlord's work, if any, and tenant's work
- The draw schedule
- What must accompany a draw: invoices, inspection sign-off, and lien waivers from the contractors who were paid
- The completion deadline
- What happens if the tenant is late: rent starts anyway, unused allowance is forfeited, or someone has a termination right

Do not fund a draw without the waivers the work letter requires. Whether a contractor's claim can attach to the property is counsel's question, and it depends on the jurisdiction and the facts. The operating rule does not: no waivers, no check. Partial opening before a certificate of occupancy creates arguments about rent start, recoveries, and insurance. Require the certificate as a condition of opening and of rent commencement, and write that in the lease.

Unused allowance is a negotiation, not a default. Landlords prefer it to expire. Tenants prefer to convert it to free rent. Decide in the LOI.

## Rent commencement and opening co-tenancy

Three dates get conflated. Delivery is when you hand over the space. Lease commencement is when the term starts. Rent commencement is when base rent is due. The gap between delivery and rent is the free-rent period, usually bought so the tenant can build. An illustrative free-rent band is one to six months, tied to the build-out, not to optimism.

Opening co-tenancy lets a tenant delay opening, or pay less, if a named anchor is dark. From the owner's side this is concentration risk: one anchor closure can move several inline rents at once. If you agree to it, name the anchors, require a stated dark period before the remedy starts (90 to 180 days is a planning range people negotiate), and cap the rent reduction. "Any anchor, immediately, rent goes to zero" is not a clause to discover in the final draft.

## How deals die after the LOI

| Failure | When it appears | What to do earlier |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Tenant cannot fund the build-out | Signature or construction | Proof of funds, or a guaranty, at the LOI |
| Use hits an existing exclusive | Drafting | Map exclusives before you issue the LOI |
| Scope outgrows the allowance | Construction | A scope in the work letter, and change orders in writing |
| Lender must approve the lease | Signature | Read the loan file for approval rights before the LOI |
| Permit or zoning delay | Construction | Confirm the use with the jurisdiction before you sign |

Walking away from a non-binding LOI may be your right and still a reputation event in a small broker market. Have a reason, and say it plainly. Do not ghost a deal you are not going to make.

## Questions during the paper

**Is the LOI binding?** Treat it as non-binding only if it says so and counsel agrees the draft does what you think. Do not rely on a general impression of local custom.

**Can we walk?** If it is non-binding, yes. Do it with a reason.

**What allowance is reasonable?** Use the planning bands above as a start, then price the actual scope and the term. Food costs more than a dry goods shop.

**They want to open early.** Do not allow a partial opening that leaves rent start, recoveries, and insurance undefined.

**Leftover allowance.** Whatever the LOI said. If the LOI is silent, you are negotiating it at the worst time.

**Whose form?** Yours. Then negotiate.

## The practical next step

1. Put a non-binding sentence on your LOI form and have counsel read it once, before the next deal.
2. Map every exclusive in the center before the next proposal goes out.
3. Write renewal rent, allowance draws, and unused-allowance treatment into the LOI, not the third redline.
4. Make lien waivers and a certificate of occupancy conditions of payment and of opening.
5. Read the loan documents for lease-approval rights before you promise a delivery date.
