# Late fees and grace periods that survive a file review

The late fee that causes trouble later is the one assessed from memory. The grace period that causes trouble is the one you extend by habit and never write down. A lender, a buyer, or a collector is not grading toughness. They are checking whether the file matches itself: the lease, the date, the amount, and the way every other tenant in the same position was treated.

This is an operating note. It is not a statute summary, and it is not a state lease or eviction article. What a notice must say, and what a given lease allows as a charge, is counsel's reading. Your job is one clock, and a worksheet that proves you ran it.

## The clock is the policy

Most retail leases make rent due on a stated day, often the first, and late after a stated grace. The weekly operating rhythm already uses that shape: post receipts as they arrive, and on the first business day after grace, work the delinquency list. The fee sits on top of that cadence. It does not replace it.

Write one page, then apply it tenant by tenant, because the clauses are not identical.

- **Due date.** The day that lease says. Not the day ACH usually lands.
- **Grace end.** The day that lease says the account is late. A longer courtesy is a waiver, and waivers get a row.
- **Fee.** The amount or the formula in that lease. If two suites say two different things, you assess two different things. You do not average them into a house number the document does not contain.
- **Who posts it, and who may waive it.** One name for each. A waiver row records the date, the amount, the reason, and the name. A verbal "don't worry about it this month" is how the real grace period drifts a week past the lease.

Tenants learn the clock you enforce. If grace ends on the fifth and you never call until the fifteenth, the fifteenth is the policy. Write that down if you mean it. Do not discover it inside a collections file.

Do not assess the fee while a payment is still inside the window you defined as not yet collected. A submitted ACH is not a late tenant. A returned ACH might be. The fee follows the return and the grace clause, in that order.

## What a file review is actually testing

A buyer or a lender sampling collections is trying to learn whether the rent is real. Late fees are a side question that becomes a main question when they are large, uneven, or doing the work of rent.

Three tests cover most of what they are looking for.

**The fee is in the document.** For a sample of charges, you can turn to the clause and show the arithmetic. A flat amount is a flat amount. A percentage is the percentage the lease states, applied to the balance the lease names, not to a round number that felt right.

**The same position got the same treatment.** Suites that were late the same way were assessed the same way, or the waiver log explains the difference. A fee that lands only on tenants you are already frustrated with will not read as a policy. It will read as a quarrel. Collectors have to live with that page. Give them a page that is boring.

**The fee is not pretending to be rent.** Book it as a fee. A waived fee is not income. Be able to put the aging beside the fees. If you need late fees to make the month look leased, the month is a collections month, and the schedule will not hide that. A good tenant can still receive a waiver. The waiver is a row, so the next person does not inherit a secret grace period.

## The practical next step

1. For each current lease, copy the due date, the grace end, and the fee formula onto one list. Mark any clause you do not understand and send that list to counsel. Do not fill the blanks from memory.
2. Assess this month only from that list, on the first business day after each grace end, and only where the rent is actually unpaid.
3. Start a waiver log with four columns: date, unit label, amount waived, who approved. Use it this month even if the first entries feel small.
4. Put the fee list next to the delinquency list in the weekly review so the two cannot drift apart.
