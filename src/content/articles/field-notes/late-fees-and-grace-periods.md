# Late fees and grace periods that survive a file review

The late fee that causes trouble later is the one assessed from memory. The grace period that causes trouble is the one you extend by habit and never write down. A lender, a buyer, or a collector is not grading how tough you are. They are checking whether the file matches itself: the lease, the date, the amount, and the way every other tenant in the same position was treated.

This is an operating note. It is not a statute summary, and it is not a state lease or eviction article. What a notice must say, and what a given lease allows as a charge, is counsel's reading. Your job is one clock, and a worksheet that proves you ran it. The weekly collections pass already exists. "The Weekly Operating Rhythm," in the Shopping Center Operator Series, posts receipts as they arrive and works the delinquency list on the first business day after grace. This note is the fee that sits on that pass, and the log that keeps the fee from becoming a second, unofficial grace period.

## The clock is the policy

Most retail leases make rent due on a stated day, often the first, and late after a stated grace. The clauses are not identical from suite to suite. A house rule that ignores the document will not survive the first time someone lays the lease next to the invoice.

Write one page, then apply it tenant by tenant.

- **Due date.** The day that lease says. Not the day ACH usually lands, and not the day the bookkeeper opens the bank.
- **Grace end.** The day that lease says the account is late. A longer courtesy is a waiver. Waivers get a row.
- **Fee.** The amount or the formula in that lease. If two suites say two different things, you assess two different things. You do not average them into a house number the document does not contain.
- **Who posts it, and who may waive it.** One name for each. A waiver row records the date, the unit label, the amount, the reason, and the name. A verbal "don't worry about it this month" is how the real grace period drifts a week past the lease.

Tenants learn the clock you enforce. If grace ends on the fifth and you never call until the fifteenth, the fifteenth is the policy. That is manageable if you meant it and wrote it as the practice. It is a poor surprise to discover inside a collections file, when the tenant's version of the deal is the version you taught them.

Do not assess the fee while a payment is still inside the window you defined as not yet collected. A submitted ACH is not a late tenant. A returned ACH might be. The fee follows the return and the grace clause, in that order. Stacking a fee on the morning you submitted the debit is how a routine draft becomes an argument. The field note "The first five days after an ACH return" is the return. This note starts when the rent is actually unpaid and grace has ended.

## One illustrative month

The table is a teaching example. The names are placeholders. None of these clauses is a claim about any live lease.

| Unit | Due | Grace ends | Fee the lease states | What happened | What you post |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| A-1 | 1st | 5th | $100 flat | Still unpaid on the morning of the 6th | $100 fee. Rent stays open. |
| A-2 | 1st | 5th | 5% of the unpaid balance | Paid in full on the 4th | No fee. |
| B-1 | 1st | 10th | $75 flat | Unpaid on the 11th. Owner waives once, bank error documented | Waiver row. No income. |
| B-2 | 1st | 5th | $100 or 5% of the unpaid balance, whichever is greater | $3,000 unpaid on the 6th | $150. Five percent of $3,000 is $150, which is greater than $100. Show both numbers. |

Read the rows as a worksheet, not as a policy you can copy onto your center.

A-1 and B-2 can both be late on the same morning and owe different fees, because the leases differ. Posting $100 on both because that is "what we charge" is the error a file review finds in ten minutes. A-2 paid inside grace. The fact that the draft was late relative to the first is irrelevant if the lease gives through the fifth. Charging A-2 anyway teaches the tenant that the clause is decorative.

B-1 is the row people skip. The owner had a reason. The reason is not the problem. The missing row is the problem. Without it, next month's bookkeeper sees a late tenant and no fee and either "corrects" the file by adding one, or inherits a secret grace period through the 11th. Write the waiver the day you grant it. Four columns are enough: date, unit label, amount waived, who approved. A fifth column for the reason is better. "Good tenant" is not a reason. "Bank error, letter from the tenant's bank in the file, one time" is a reason.

B-2 is where the formula has to be visible. The lease takes the greater of $100 and 5%. On a $3,000 balance that is $150, and the ledger note shows both figures. A collector who inherits the balance should not have to reverse-engineer your rounding. If you cannot show the arithmetic from the clause, do not post the fee. Send the clause to counsel and wait.

## What a file review is actually testing

A buyer or a lender sampling collections is trying to learn whether the rent is real. Late fees are a side question that becomes the main question when they are large, uneven, or doing the work of rent. Three tests cover most of what they ask. You can run the same three tests on your own file before anyone else does.

**The fee is in the document.** For a sample of charges, you can turn to the clause and show the arithmetic. A flat amount is a flat amount. A percentage is the percentage the lease states, applied to the balance the lease names, not to a round number that felt right. Pull five fees from the last quarter. Next to each, put the section and the one line of math. If you cannot do it for two of the five, the practice is memory, and memory will not travel with the file.

**The same position got the same treatment.** Suites that were late the same way were assessed the same way, or the waiver log explains the difference. A fee that lands only on tenants you are already frustrated with will not read as a policy. It will read as a quarrel. Collectors have to live with that page. Give them a page that is boring. Boring is the compliment. It means a stranger can apply next month's fees without calling you.

**The fee is not pretending to be rent.** Book it as a fee. A waived fee is not income. Be able to put the aging beside the fees. If a month's "other income" is mostly late fees, and the delinquency list is quiet, you will be asked which of those two pages is true. This note is not a lesson in rebuilding income or a trailing-twelve. It is the reminder that fees are a collections signal. If you need them to make the month look leased, the month is a collections month, and the schedule will not hide that.

What you hand a collector is a thin packet: the fee clause, the ledger with rent and fees on separate lines, the waiver rows for that unit, and the dates you already wrote down. You hand them a clock that already ran.

## Where the fee goes in the books

Rent is rent. A late fee is other income, or whatever line your accountant has named for it, and it is dated the day you assessed it. Do not fold it into the rent charge so the row looks like one number. A later waiver, a later payment of rent only, and a later question from a buyer all get harder when the fee has disappeared into the rent line.

If the tenant pays the fee, clear the fee. If the tenant pays the rent and not the fee, the fee stays open. Do not apply a rent payment to the fee first unless the lease says that is the order, and unless counsel has told you to follow that order. Silent application is how a tenant who thinks they are current discovers a fee balance from four months ago. That discovery usually arrives in the middle of a renewal.

A model can list this month's open rows, compare them to the fee worksheet, and draft the one-line ledger note that shows the arithmetic. A person posts the fee and signs the waiver. The model does not decide that a clause means what the office has always done, and it does not waive anyone.

## The practical next step

1. For each current lease, copy the due date, the grace end, and the fee formula onto one list. Mark any clause you do not understand and send that list to counsel. Do not fill the blanks from memory.
2. Assess this month only from that list, on the first business day after each grace end, and only where the rent is actually unpaid.
3. Start a waiver log with date, unit label, amount waived, reason, and who approved. Use it this month even if the first entry feels small.
4. Pull five fees from last quarter and put the lease section and the arithmetic beside each one. Fix the ones you cannot show.
5. Put the fee list next to the delinquency list in the weekly review so the two cannot drift apart.
