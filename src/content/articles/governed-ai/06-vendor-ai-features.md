# Vendor AI Features: Questions to Ask Before Your Software Turns Them On

Most owner-led businesses will not buy their first AI tool. It will arrive as a new button in software they already use: the accounting package, the scheduling system, the email client, the document platform. Some of these features are useful. Some read more of your data than the old product did. Some are switched on by default. This article gives you ten questions to ask before a vendor AI feature touches your work, and a decision table that turns the answers into one of three outcomes: switch it on, pilot it, or leave it off for now.

## AI that arrives by update

A standalone AI tool goes through a buying decision. Someone evaluates it, someone approves the spend, and someone decides where it fits. A vendor AI feature often skips all of that. It appears after an update, with a short announcement and a toggle. Sometimes the toggle is already on.

That is not a reason for alarm. Vendors add features because customers ask for them, and many are sensible: a summary of a long email thread, a suggested category for an expense, a draft reply to a routine request. The issue is that a feature switched on without a decision is an AI use nobody wrote down. It does not appear in the AI use register. Nobody has set its level. Nobody knows what data it reads.

The fix is not to refuse every feature. It is to give vendor features the same short review you would give a new tool, before they touch real work. "The AI Use Register: Writing Down Where AI Touches the Business" describes where the result gets recorded. This article covers the questions that come first.

## Ten questions before you switch it on

Ask these of the vendor, the vendor's documentation, or your own team. Write the answers down. An answer of "unknown" is a finding, not a failure.

### What it does

1. **What does the feature produce?** A summary, a draft, a suggested category, a ranked list, an automatic change to a record. Name the output in one line.
2. **At which level does it operate?** Use the three levels from "Prepare, Recommend, Decide: Three Levels of AI Involvement and Who Signs Each." A feature that drafts is at prepare. A feature that proposes an option for a person to accept is at recommend. A feature that changes a record, sends a message, or releases a payment without a person acting is trying to hold the decide level, which AI never holds. That feature needs a person inserted before it, or it stays off.
3. **Which workflow step does it touch?** Point to the row on your workflow map. If you cannot, the feature may not serve a real step.

### What it sees

4. **What data does it read?** One record, one customer's file, or everything in the account. "Data In, Data Out: What an AI Tool Should and Should Not See" covers how to scope this.
5. **Where does that data go?** Whether the vendor sends it to another provider, whether it is kept, and for how long. Record what the vendor states. Where contracts or regulation may govern how that data is handled, confirm your obligations with qualified advisors before switching the feature on.
6. **Can access be limited by role?** A feature that respects the permissions you already set is easier to govern than one that reads across them.

### How you control it

7. **Who can switch it on or off?** An administrator, every user, or only the vendor.
8. **Does it keep a record?** Whether you can see what the feature produced and what a person changed, as described in "The Audit Trail: Recording What AI Prepared and What a Person Changed."
9. **How will you know when it changes?** Whether the vendor announces updates to the feature or its underlying model. This question matters again in the next article in this series.
10. **What happens if you turn it off later?** Whether your records, settings, and history stay intact.

## A decision table for vendor features

The answers sort each feature into one of three outcomes. The table is a starting point for the owner or process owner, not a rule.

| Level of the output | Data it reads | Record and control | Outcome |
|---|---|---|---|
| Prepare, internal use only | Limited to the record in front of the user | You can see output and switch it off | Switch on. Add to the register. Spot-check monthly. |
| Prepare or recommend, reaches outside parties | Limited, or respects roles | You can see output and edits | Pilot with one team for a month, with review before anything leaves the building. |
| Recommend, touches money, dates, or terms | Broad or unclear | Partial record | Pilot only after the data questions are answered. Named reviewer on every item. |
| Acts without a person (sends, changes, releases) | Any | Any | Leave off, or configure so a person acts first. |
| Any level | Unknown | Unknown | Leave off until the vendor answers questions 4, 5, and 8. |

Three illustrative cases show the table at work.

- **A regional title office**, used here as an illustrative example, finds that its document platform now offers AI summaries of uploaded files. The summaries are prepare-level, stay internal, and read only the file in view. The office switches the feature on, adds it to the register, and has the closer spot-check summaries against the documents each week.
- **A hypothetical landscaping company** sees that its scheduling software can now reschedule crews automatically when weather changes and text customers the new date. That feature acts without a person. The owner leaves auto-send off and uses the feature only to propose a revised schedule, which the operations manager approves before any customer hears about it.
- **An illustrative property manager** finds that its accounting software suggests expense categories and can auto-post them. Suggestions stay on. Auto-posting stays off until a month of suggestions has been reviewed and the correction rate is known.

## Scope discipline is a feature decision

The most useful question on the list is the third one: which step does this feature touch? Vendors build for a wide market. A feature can be well made and still serve none of your actual work. Every feature you switch on adds something to review, something to explain to new staff, and something that can change without notice.

Saying no is a legitimate outcome. It is not a judgment on the vendor or on AI. It is a statement that this feature does not serve a step your business performs, or that you are not yet ready to review what it produces. A feature left off can be revisited next quarter.

### The view from Arnould Blvd

Command Platform exists because generic property software handled most of a real shopping center and dropped the rest: the recorded instruments, the suite-by-suite obligations, the parking constraints that govern leasing. Building a system around On The Blvd Shopping Center meant saying no constantly. There was no portfolio abstraction, no generic unit types, and no feature that did not serve the property's actual work. The same discipline applies to AI. The platform's AI concierge was added with a narrow scope: it reads the property's own records, summarizes, points to the governing document, and drafts for review. It does not negotiate, approve, or send. When a vendor switches on an AI feature in software you already use, the owner's job is the same one: ask what step it serves, keep what does, and leave the rest off.

## Keeping the list current

Vendor features change faster than standalone tools, because they ride along with routine updates. Build a light habit around them.

- **Name an owner for each product.** One person reads the release notes for each major piece of software and flags any new AI feature.
- **Check defaults after updates.** Some features arrive switched on. A quick look at the settings after a major update catches them.
- **Record the decision.** Whether a feature is on, piloting, or off, write it in the register with the date and the reason. A future reviewer will want to know why.
- **Revisit quarterly.** A feature left off for good reasons may earn a pilot later, once your data is scoped or your review points are in place.

A hypothetical twelve-person engineering firm might do all of this in a thirty-minute quarterly meeting: the office manager lists new AI features across its five main products, the principal decides on each, and the register is updated before the meeting ends.

## The practical next step

1. List the software your business pays for and name one person responsible for watching each product's AI features.
2. Open the settings of your three most-used products and note every AI feature that is already switched on.
3. Run each of those features through the ten questions and record the answers, including every "unknown."
4. Use the decision table to set an outcome for each: switch on, pilot, or leave off. Turn off any feature that acts without a person.
5. Add every feature that stays on to your AI use register, with its level, its reviewer, and the date of the decision.
