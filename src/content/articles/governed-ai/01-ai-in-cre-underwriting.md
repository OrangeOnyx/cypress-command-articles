# AI in CRE Underwriting: What the Model Prepares and What the Owner Signs

An AI tool can read a rent roll, an offering memorandum, and a stack of leases and return a tidy underwriting summary before lunch, but that speed does not close the gap between what the model read and what the buyer is actually buying. This article opens the Governed AI Series with a boundary worksheet: one table that splits an underwriting file into what the model prepares and what the owner verifies and signs.

## What underwriting asks, and what a model can do with it

Underwriting is the work of deciding what a property can support: the income it produces, the costs it carries, the risks attached to both, and the price and terms that follow. In a commercial real estate acquisition it usually starts with documents the seller provides. There is a rent roll, the list of tenants, suites, rents, and lease dates. There is an operating statement, copies of the leases, and an offering memorandum, which is the seller's marketing package.

A language model is useful for a specific slice of that work. It can extract lease dates into a table. It can compare the rent roll to the leases and flag suites where the figures disagree. It can summarize a forty-page lease into the terms an analyst needs. It can draft the first pass of an investment memo from numbers someone has already checked. Every one of those is preparation: reading, extracting, comparing, organizing, drafting.

What a model cannot do is know what it was not given. It works from the documents in front of it. If a seller's package leaves out a recorded restriction, the model's summary leaves it out too, in the same confident tone it uses for everything else. The summary looks complete because it is well organized. Completeness is a separate question, and it is the owner's question.

That is the boundary this series is about. Governance, as the word is used here, is not a product or a certificate. It is five practical things an owner can run: written boundaries, named owners, review, records, and a cadence for checking again. Underwriting is a good place to start because the cost of a missing fact is large and the signature at the end is unmistakably the owner's.

## Three illustrative files, one pattern

Consider a hypothetical buyer looking at a neighborhood retail center. The seller provides a rent roll and square footage. The model reads both, applies the rent assumptions the analyst supplied, and projects income if two vacant suites are leased to restaurants. The math is clean. The conclusion is wrong if the center's zoning approval ties floor space to parking and restaurants park more intensively than retail. Nothing in the rent roll mentions parking. The model did its job. The file was incomplete.

Now set real estate aside. This series uses one running example throughout, described here as illustrative: a regional title office, with a handful of examiners, processors, and closers handling residential and small commercial closings. Imagine the owner of that office weighing whether to buy a smaller competitor's book of business. An AI tool reads the competitor's order history and fee schedules and produces a clean revenue projection. What it did not read: the referral relationships that follow one escrow officer who is leaving, and a lender panel arrangement that may not carry over to a new owner. The pattern is the same. The model summarized what it was handed. The owner has to ask what it was not handed.

A third, briefer case: a hypothetical HVAC company buying a competitor's maintenance agreements. The AI tallies the contracts and their annual revenue. Whether those agreements can be assigned is a question for counsel. Whether the customers will stay is a question for the owner. Neither answer lives in the spreadsheet.

In each file, the model's output is a reasonable starting point. In each file, the decision rests on facts that sit outside the documents the model read.

## The boundary worksheet

The tool for this article is a single table. List every input to the evaluation, then fill in four columns. The illustrative entries below use a small retail acquisition. The same structure works for buying a service book, a practice, or a route.

| Underwriting input | What the model may prepare | What the owner verifies, and against what | Who signs |
|---|---|---|---|
| Rent roll | Extract tenants, suites, rents, and dates into a table; flag gaps | Each line against the executed lease and its amendments | Owner or a named analyst |
| Leases | Summarize term, options, exclusives, and co-tenancy; list dates | Any clause that affects income or use, read in full by a person; legal meaning by counsel | Owner, with counsel where needed |
| Operating statement | Organize by category; compare years; flag large swings | Unusual items against invoices and the seller's explanation | Owner |
| Site and land | Summarize the survey and title documents provided | Recorded constraints: plat, zoning approvals, easements, parcels not included in the sale | Owner, with surveyor and counsel |
| Market assumptions | Format a range from inputs the analyst supplied | The assumptions themselves, which the model does not supply | Owner |
| Investment memo | Draft from checked figures | Every figure traced to a verified source; conclusions written by a person | Owner |

Three rules keep the worksheet honest.

- **The model never supplies an assumption.** Rent growth, vacancy, and exit pricing are the owner's judgment. A model can lay out a range the analyst gives it. It should not invent one.
- **Every verified figure names its source.** "Checked against the executed lease, second amendment" is a verification. "Looks right" is not.
- **The site and land row is never blank.** It is the row most likely to be thin in a seller's package, and the row most likely to change what the property can support.

For a business outside real estate, rename the row rather than drop it. In the title office example it becomes "relationships and agreements": who the business depends on, and which arrangements may not survive a change of owner.

## What gets recorded

A boundary only holds if someone can see where it was drawn. For each underwriting file, keep three records beside the worksheet: the documents the model was given, the output it produced, and the changes a person made before the figures went into the memo. That is not a heavy archive. It is a folder with the inputs, the draft, and a short note of what changed and why.

The record serves the owner first. When a lender, partner, or co-investor asks where a number came from, the answer is in the file. It also serves the next acquisition. If the change notes show that the model regularly mismatches rent roll dates with lease amendments, the analyst knows where to look harder next time.

The same discipline applies in the title office. If the owner uses an AI tool to evaluate the competitor's book, the file should show which records the tool read, which projection it produced, and which assumptions the owner changed after talking with the departing escrow officer. Later articles in this series build out each piece: a register of AI uses, levels of involvement, the data a tool should see, and the audit trail.

### The view from Arnould Blvd

The property that anchors this series is On The Blvd Shopping Center, a legacy multi-tenant center at 101–149 Arnould Blvd in Lafayette, roughly 63,000 SF on 4.84 acres, run with Command Platform. It illustrates the gap in the first example exactly. Its recorded parking variance, Entry 99-11797, provides 324 spaces against 344 required and limits floor space to available parking. A model that reads rent and square footage but not that variance would see leasable area and project income on all of it. It would not see that the approval ties that area to a parking count, so a use that parks harder than retail cannot simply be penciled in. It would overstate what the site can support. The same caution applies to the land. The corner bank parcel was sold and is not part of the center, even though it sits inside the block's outline, and a summary built from an aerial image or a loose description could count it. The owner signs, so the owner checks the recorded constraints. "Parking Is the Lease," in the Shopping Center Operator Series, walks through how.

## Where the line moves, and where it does not

Owners will reasonably ask whether the boundary loosens as tools improve. Parts of it can. The extraction step may need less rework over time, and the change notes will show whether it does. Lightening that review is a decision for the owner, made deliberately and written down.

Other parts of the line do not move. The model does not choose assumptions. It does not decide what the property is worth. It does not approve the purchase, sign the letter of intent, or send anything to a seller, broker, or lender. Where legal meaning matters, counsel reads the document. Where regulation may apply to the transaction or to the information involved, the business confirms its obligations with qualified advisors. None of that reflects distrust of the tool. It reflects who is accountable when the file closes.

## The practical next step

1. Take the most recent acquisition, expansion, or major contract you evaluated, and list every input to that decision.
2. Copy the boundary worksheet and fill in the four columns for each input, writing "none" wherever no verification happened.
3. Add a site and land row, or its equivalent in your business: the recorded or contractual constraints a seller's package might leave out.
4. Set up a folder structure for the next evaluation: inputs, model output, and a change note.
5. Write one sentence stating what AI may not do in your evaluations, and share it with everyone who prepares figures for you.
