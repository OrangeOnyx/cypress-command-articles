# The AI Use Register: Writing Down Where AI Touches the Business

Most owner-led businesses already use AI in more places than anyone has listed: a feature switched on in the accounting software, a chat tool an assistant uses for drafts, a transcription app in internal meetings. This article gives you the AI use register, a single table with one row per use, and shows how to fill it in, find the uses nobody mentioned, and keep it current.

## Why write it down

You cannot set a boundary around something nobody has named. The first governance step is a plain inventory: where does AI read your information, produce something, or suggest a course of action? Until that list exists, every other control in this series has nothing to attach to.

The register is not a policy and it is not a risk assessment. It is a record of fact. Each row says what a use is, what it touches, who owns it, and what it may not do. An owner who keeps a vehicle list or a list of software subscriptions already knows the shape.

The difference is that AI uses tend to arrive quietly. Many arrive inside software the business already pays for, switched on by an update. Others arrive through a person solving a real problem with a tool they found on their own. Neither is misconduct. Both are uses the business should know about, because each one reads something and produces something, and someone should be accountable for both ends.

A register also changes the conversation with the team. The question stops being "are we allowed to use AI?" and becomes "is this use on the register, and does it have an owner?" People can answer that question, and the answer leads somewhere.

## The ten columns

Each row in the register is one use: one tool doing one kind of job in one workflow. If the same tool does two different jobs, it gets two rows, because the two jobs may read different information and need different reviewers.

| Column | Question it answers | What a good entry looks like |
|---|---|---|
| Use | What does the AI do, and with which tool? | "Extracts requirements from search packages (feature in the title software)" |
| Workflow step | Where in the work does it sit? | "Examination, after the search is returned" |
| Reads | What information is it given, and how much? | "The search package for the open file; nothing else" |
| Produces | What comes out? | "A draft list of requirements" |
| Level | Prepare, recommend, or decide? | "Prepare" |
| Owner | Who is accountable for this use existing and working? | A role, plus a name while the register is being built |
| Reviewer | Who checks the output before it is used? | "Examiner on the file; senior examiner on commercial files" |
| May not | What is this use forbidden to do? | "May not finalize the commitment or send anything outside the office" |
| Record | Where are the output and the review kept? | "File notes in the title software, with reviewer initials" |
| Next review | When will someone look at this row again? | A date, not "periodically" |

The Level column uses three terms defined in the next article, "Prepare, Recommend, Decide: Three Levels of AI Involvement and Who Signs Each." The short version is enough for now. Prepare means the AI produces material a person works from. Recommend means it proposes a choice that a person makes. Decide is reserved for people, so a row that shows AI at decide is a finding to fix, not a category to fill.

Four rules keep the register useful.

- **"May not" is never blank.** Every use has limits. Writing them down is the boundary.
- **Owner and reviewer are both named.** They can be the same person in a small business. They cannot be "the team."
- **Unknowns are entries.** If nobody knows what a vendor feature reads, write "unknown" and make finding out a task with a name and a date.
- **One register for the business.** A register per department drifts. One table, sorted by workflow, stays readable.

## A worked register for an illustrative title office

The following example is illustrative. The regional title office used throughout this series builds its first register over two weeks. Four of its rows appear below, with the workflow step, record, and next review columns left out for width.

| Use | Reads | Produces | Level | Owner | Reviewer | May not |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Search package extraction (title software feature) | Search package for the open file | Draft requirements list | Prepare | Examination lead | Examiner on the file | Finalize the commitment; send outside the office |
| Payoff letter comparison (same software) | Payoff letters and lender figures for the file | List of mismatches | Prepare | Closing manager | Closer | Change any figure; contact the lender |
| Status reply drafting (general chat tool used by processors) | Text a processor pastes in | Draft replies to agents and lenders | Prepare | Operations manager | The processor who sends it | Receive account numbers, payoff figures, or identification documents; send anything itself |
| Meeting notes (transcription app used by the owner) | Audio of internal staff meetings | Summary and action list | Prepare | Owner | Owner | Be used on customer or lender calls; share summaries outside the office |

Read the register and it starts to show things.

The first two rows were switched on by the software vendor in an update. Nobody at the office decided to use them. Now someone has, on the record, with a reviewer.

The third row was found by asking, not by checking software. A processor had been using a general chat tool to tidy routine replies. The use is sensible. The register gives it an owner and, more importantly, a written limit on what goes into it. That limit reflects what the office chooses. Whether any regulation also applies to that information is a question the office should confirm with qualified advisors.

Every row sits at prepare. That is typical for a first register and a sound place to start.

## Finding the uses nobody mentioned

A register is only as complete as the search behind it. Three sources cover most of the ground.

1. **Software settings and release notes.** Walk through each system the business uses and look for features labeled assistant, smart, summary, suggested, or AI. Vendors often announce these in release notes that nobody reads.
2. **The team.** Ask a short, plain question: "What tools do you use to draft, summarize, or look something up?" Say clearly that the goal is a list, not discipline. People share more when the first answer is not punished.
3. **Invoices and card statements.** Subscriptions and add-ons show up in spending before they show up anywhere else.

Uses outside the title office look the same. A hypothetical twelve-person HVAC company might find that its dispatch software now suggests the order of the day's service calls. That row sits at recommend, because the dispatcher chooses whether to follow the suggestion. A hypothetical accounting practice might find that its document portal summarizes uploaded client files. That row sits at prepare. Both belong on the register the week they are found.

### The view from Arnould Blvd

The AI concierge in Command Platform at On The Blvd can be written up as a single register row. It shows what a complete entry looks like for a use that sits close to real documents.

| Column | Entry |
|---|---|
| Use | Answers questions and drafts against the property's records (Command Platform AI concierge) |
| Reads | The property's own records, assembled by the system: lease terms, vendor records, and the property's documents. Not the open internet. |
| Produces | Summaries of a clause, a pointer to the governing document, first-pass notice drafts |
| Level | Prepare |
| Reviewer | The operator, and the owner where the matter calls for it |
| May not | Negotiate, approve, or send |

The row is short because the boundary is clear. Every column has an answer, and the "may not" column names the three actions that carry the most consequence.

## Keeping the register current

A register written once and filed goes stale within a season. Keep it alive with an owner and a cadence.

- **Name a register owner.** One person keeps the table, adds rows, and runs the review. In a small business this is often the owner or an operations manager.
- **Set a cadence.** A short monthly look for new rows, and a fuller quarterly review of every row's reviewer, limits, and next review date, is a reasonable starting rhythm. Adjust it to how fast your tools change.
- **Add triggers.** A new tool, a vendor update announcing AI features, a new kind of information going into an existing tool, a change in reviewer, or an error that reached someone outside the business: each one prompts a row review before the next scheduled date.
- **Retire rows on the record.** When a use stops, mark it retired with the date. Do not delete it. The history is part of the record.

## The practical next step

1. Copy the ten-column table into a spreadsheet and name one person as the register owner.
2. Walk the settings of your three most-used business systems and list every AI feature you find, including ones you have never used.
3. Ask each team member the drafting and summarizing question, and add every answer as a row.
4. Fill in "May not" for every row before you fill in anything else that is missing.
5. Put the first quarterly register review on the calendar, with the register owner running it.
