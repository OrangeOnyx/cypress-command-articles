# Prepare, Recommend, Decide: Three Levels of AI Involvement and Who Signs Each

Saying that AI helps with a task hides the question that matters: how close does it get to the decision? This article defines three levels of involvement, gives a short test for placing any use at the right level, and shows who signs at each one. The levels fill the Level column of the AI use register and carry through every article that follows.

## Why levels, not yes or no

Asking "can we use AI for this?" tends to produce an argument. Asking "at what level?" tends to produce an answer, because it points at the specific step and the specific person who is accountable for it.

The same tool can sit at different levels in different steps. Take a hypothetical twelve-person HVAC company. When its software drafts a job summary from a technician's notes, the AI is preparing material. When the software ranks the day's service calls by urgency, it is recommending an order. When the office commits a same-day appointment to a customer, a decision has been made, and someone at the company owns it. One tool, three levels, three different review needs.

Levels also make the register readable at a glance. An owner scanning the Level column can see how far AI reaches into the business without reading every row.

## The three levels defined

| Level | What the AI does | Typical verbs | What the person does | Who signs | Minimum record |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Prepare | Produces material a person works from. Favors no option. | Summarize, extract, organize, compare, draft, flag | Reviews, corrects, and uses the material in their own work | The person who uses the material | Input, output, and the reviewer's changes |
| Recommend | Proposes a choice among options, with its reasons and sources | Rank, score, suggest, propose | Weighs the proposal against the alternatives and chooses | The named decision owner | The recommendation, the choice made, and the reason when they differ |
| Decide | Nothing on its own. AI may prepare or recommend for this step, but the step itself belongs to a person. | None | Makes the call that commits the business and takes responsibility for it | The named decision owner, every time | The decision, who made it, when, and on what material |

In plain terms:

- **Prepare.** The output is raw material. Nothing in it is a choice. If the output were deleted, no decision would have been made, only some reading and drafting lost. Extracting dates from a contract, summarizing a file, drafting a routine reply, and flagging a figure that does not match are all prepare.
- **Recommend.** The output points toward an answer. A person can accept it, change it, or reject it, and should have the options and sources in view when they do. Recommend is where AI influence is highest relative to how visible it is, because a ranked list shapes a choice even when a person makes the final call.
- **Decide.** The step commits the business: money, a price, an approval, a promise, a notice, or anything sent outside the building. In this series, decide is a human level by definition. AI never holds it. A register row showing AI at decide is a finding, and the fix is to move the use down a level or put a named person back into the step.

## A placement test

Run every register row through three questions, in order.

1. **Does the output take effect without a person acting?** If it sends, approves, pays, commits, or changes a record of consequence on its own, the use is sitting at decide. Stop and redesign it so a named person acts before anything takes effect.
2. **Does the output favor one option over others?** A ranking, a score, a suggested choice, or a proposed price is recommend.
3. **Otherwise, it is prepare.** Summaries, extractions, comparisons, drafts, and flags stay at prepare.

Then one check for every use, at any level: could the reviewer tell if the output were wrong? If the source is not beside the draft, the review has no footing, and the use needs that fixed before the level means anything.

Applied to the regional title office used throughout this series, described here as illustrative, the test sorts the work like this.

| Task | Level | Who signs |
|---|---|---|
| Summarize a returned search package | Prepare | Examiner who uses the summary |
| Flag mismatches between payoff letters and lender figures | Prepare | Closer |
| Draft a routine status reply to a real estate agent | Prepare | Processor who sends it |
| Suggest which requirements belong on the commitment | Recommend | Examiner, who decides and records any difference |
| Rank open files by likelihood of a delayed closing | Recommend | Closing manager |
| Issue the commitment, approve closing figures, release funds | Decide | A named person, every time; AI's part ends at preparing material |

## Who signs, and what signing means

Signing means a person accepts responsibility for the output as used. They read it. They checked what the review scope requires them to check. The record shows their name.

At **prepare**, the signer is the person who uses the material. An examiner who relies on an AI summary of a search package is signing for that summary when the requirements go on the commitment. If the summary missed something, the review is where it should have been caught.

At **recommend**, the signer is the named decision owner, and the record needs one extra field: what the person chose, and why, when it differs from the recommendation. That field does two jobs. It shows the person was weighing the proposal, not just accepting it. Over time it also shows whether the recommendations are useful. If the owner of the decision never diverges, either the recommendations are very good or the person has stopped looking. A sample check will tell you which. "Where Human Review Belongs in an AI-Enabled Workflow," in the Operating Systems Series, covers how to set the depth of that review.

At **decide**, the signer is the named decision owner, always. No AI output substitutes for that signature, however good the preparation behind it.

## Watch for level creep

Uses drift upward when nobody is looking. A drafted reply that goes out unedited for months becomes, in practice, a message the AI sends. A ranked list that is always followed becomes, in practice, a decision the AI makes. Nobody chose the change. It happened through habit.

Consider a hypothetical building-supply distributor whose quoting software now suggests a discount on each quote. The suggestion is recommend-level by design. After a busy season, the sales team accepts every suggested discount without adjustment. The register still says recommend. The work says decide.

Signs of creep to watch for:

- Reviewers approving output without changes over a long stretch.
- Output reaching customers, vendors, or lenders without passing through a named reviewer.
- People describing a choice as "what the system said."
- A recommend-level use with no record of anyone ever choosing differently.

The fix is procedural, not technical. A use's level changes only when its owner changes it, on the record, in the register. Check each row's level at the scheduled register review, and compare the level on paper to the level in practice.

### The view from Arnould Blvd

The AI concierge in Command Platform at On The Blvd sits at prepare. It summarizes a clause, points to the governing document, and drafts a first-pass notice for review. Its role is to prepare and point. It does not negotiate, approve, or send. The decisions that follow from its preparation stay with the operator, and with the owner where the matter calls for it. Keeping the concierge at prepare is a choice with a practical payoff: every output is material a person works from, so the review question is always the same one. Does the draft match the document it points to?

## The practical next step

1. Open your AI use register and fill in the Level column for every row using the three-question placement test.
2. For any row that lands at decide, name the person who will act before anything takes effect, and change the workflow this month.
3. For every recommend-level row, add a field to the record for the choice made and the reason when it differs from the recommendation.
4. Pick one prepare-level use and confirm the reviewer can see the source beside the output.
5. At your next register review, compare each row's level on paper to how the work actually runs, and correct whichever one is wrong.
