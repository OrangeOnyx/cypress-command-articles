# A One-Page AI Policy an Owner-Led Business Can Actually Follow

Many AI policies fail by being too long. They run to a dozen pages, borrow language from larger companies, and end up in a shared drive nobody opens. An owner-led business needs something different: a single page that the team can read in five minutes, that names real people, and that points to the working tools behind it. This article closes the Governed AI Series by gathering its terms into eight clauses. Each clause is one or two sentences. Each points to the tool from an earlier article that makes it work.

## What a policy is for

A policy is not the governance. The governance is the register, the review points, the audit trail, and the calendar. The policy is the page that tells everyone those things exist and who owns them.

That distinction keeps the page short. It does not need to explain how to build an AI use register, because "The AI Use Register: Writing Down Where AI Touches the Business" does that. It does not need to define every level of AI involvement in detail, because "Prepare, Recommend, Decide: Three Levels of AI Involvement and Who Signs Each" does that. The policy states the rule and names the tool.

A good one-page policy has four properties.

- **It is short enough to read.** If a new hire cannot read it on their first day, it is too long.
- **It names people.** Every clause that requires something names the role that owns it, and the policy lists who holds each role today.
- **It points to working tools.** Every rule has a place where it is carried out: a register, a trail, a calendar.
- **It says what it is not.** It does not claim that following it satisfies any outside requirement.

On that last point: where laws, regulations, professional rules, or contracts may apply to how a business uses AI or handles data, the business should confirm its obligations with qualified advisors. The policy described here is an operating document. It helps an owner see and run the work. It is not a substitute for that advice.

## The eight clauses

The table below is the template. The left column names the clause. The middle column gives sample language an owner can adapt. The right column points to the tool that carries it out.

| Clause | Sample language | Working tool |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Purpose | "We use AI to prepare and organize work so our people can spend more time on judgment. People remain accountable for every decision." | This page |
| 2. The register | "Every AI use in the business is written in the AI use register before it touches real work. An unlisted use is switched off until it is listed." | The AI use register |
| 3. The three levels | "AI may prepare or recommend. A named person decides. AI never holds the decide level." | Prepare, recommend, decide |
| 4. Data in, data out | "Each AI use sees only the records its task needs, scoped by role. Information we would not publish or share stays out of tools that are not scoped for it." | The data scope in each register entry |
| 5. Review | "Anything that leaves the building, or involves money, dates, or terms, is reviewed by a named person before it is used or sent." | Review points in the workflow map |
| 6. Records | "We keep what AI produced, what a person changed, who approved it, and the source they checked." | The seven-field audit record |
| 7. Vendor features | "No AI feature in our existing software is switched on until it has been through the ten questions and recorded in the register." | The vendor feature checklist |
| 8. Change and cadence | "After any model or tool change, and at least quarterly, we run the reference set and review this policy." | The reference set and the calendar |

Beneath the table, the page lists the people: the **policy owner** (usually the owner or a principal), the **process owner** for each AI use, the **reviewers**, and the **product owner** who watches each piece of software. That list is the part that changes most often, so date it.

### Why the three levels sit in the middle

Clause 3 carries more weight than the others. Prepare means AI assembles or drafts for a person. Recommend means AI proposes an option a person accepts or rejects. Decide means a named person makes and owns the call. Every other clause depends on that line being clear. The register records the level. The review clause sets checking by level. The audit record names the person who decided. If a team member remembers one sentence from the page, it should be this one.

## A worked example at a regional title office

The following example is illustrative. A regional title office, the running example in this series, adapts the template in a single afternoon. Its page reads roughly like this.

- **Purpose.** AI prepares and organizes closing work. Closers, examiners, and the owner remain accountable for every file.
- **Register.** Three AI uses are listed: document summaries in the file platform, closing checklist preparation from payoff letters, and draft replies to routine status emails.
- **Levels.** All three uses are at prepare. None recommends. None decides.
- **Data.** Each use reads only the open file. The status-email tool cannot see payoff figures.
- **Review.** Every closing checklist is reviewed by the closer against the source letters. Every status email is read before sending.
- **Records.** The audit record is kept in the file platform for checklists and in a shared log for emails.
- **Vendor features.** The office manager owns the review of new features in the file platform and the email client.
- **Change and cadence.** The office runs a fifteen-question reference set quarterly and after any announced model change. The owner reviews the page each January.

It fits on one page with room left for the list of names.

The same template adapts to other owner-led businesses. A hypothetical HVAC service company might list two uses, technician note summaries and customer appointment confirmations, and set review before release on the confirmations only. An illustrative five-person accounting practice might list AI-drafted engagement summaries and reconciliation notes, and require a partner's review on every reconciliation note because it touches client figures. Imagine a small manufacturer that uses AI to draft supplier emails and to compare purchase orders against invoices. Its page might add one line to the data clause: pricing agreements stay out of the email drafting tool. The clauses stay the same. The entries change.

## Keeping one page to one page

A policy grows by addition. Each incident, each new tool, and each question from a partner adds a paragraph. Within a year the page is five pages and nobody reads it. Resist that.

- **Put detail in the tools.** A new AI use goes in the register, not the policy. A new review rule goes in the workflow map.
- **Change clauses rarely.** The eight clauses should hold for years. What changes is the register and the list of names.
- **Date every revision.** The page shows when it was last reviewed and by whom.
- **Read it aloud once a year.** The owner reads it to the team at the annual review. If any sentence needs explaining, rewrite that sentence.

### The view from Arnould Blvd

On The Blvd Shopping Center is owned by Belle Realty of Lafayette, LLC and run under the Operator Landlord approach: active partnership with tenants, not passive rent collection. That approach depends on people staying accountable for what the property promises. Its AI concierge follows a one-sentence version of the policy in this article. It answers against the property's own records, summarizes a clause, finds the governing document, and drafts a first-pass notice for review, and it does not negotiate, approve, or send. The public description of Command Platform puts the rule plainly: the concierge "reads everything, decides nothing." A one-page policy is that sentence with the owners, the records, and the calendar written in. If an owner-led business can state its own rule that plainly, the rest of the page is bookkeeping.

## What the series adds up to

The Governed AI Series began with a boundary in "AI in CRE Underwriting: What the Model Prepares and What the Owner Signs." It wrote each AI use down in a register, set three levels of involvement, scoped what each tool may see, kept a record of what AI prepared and what a person changed, asked hard questions of vendor features, and built a reference set to catch a changing model. Each of those is a working tool. The one-page policy is the index to them.

None of it requires a large team. It requires an owner who decides where AI belongs, names the people who check it, and puts the review on a calendar.

## The practical next step

1. Copy the eight-clause template and adapt the sample language to your business in one sitting.
2. Fill in the list of names: the policy owner, a process owner for each AI use, the reviewers, and a product owner for each major piece of software.
3. Check that every clause points to a working tool that exists today. Where one does not, build that tool before publishing the page.
4. Read the page to your team and ask each person to name the AI uses they touch and the level each one sits at.
5. Put the annual policy review and the quarterly reference-set run on the calendar before the month ends.
