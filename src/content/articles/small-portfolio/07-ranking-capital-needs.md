# Ranking Capital Needs You Can't All Fund This Year

Every portfolio carries more capital needs than one year's budget can cover. Roofs, equipment, parking surfaces, systems, and signs all arrive with good reasons attached, and the loudest request or the cheapest one often wins by default. This article gives you a two-step tool: a constraint check that every project passes before it is scored, and a weighted rubric that ranks what remains, with an illustrative worked example you can copy.

## Why a list is not yet a plan

Capital requests reach an owner from every direction. A site manager flags a failing unit. A vendor recommends a replacement during a service call. A tenant or a staff member asks for an improvement. An inspection report lists items. Each request is reasonable on its own terms, and each arrives with its own urgency.

Without a common method, three patterns tend to appear. The request with the most persistent advocate moves up. The inexpensive items get approved because they are easy to say yes to, and they quietly use the budget. The large, unglamorous item, the one whose cost grows every year it waits, keeps sliding to next year.

A ranking method does not remove judgment. It gives judgment a structure. Every request is described the same way, checked against the same constraints, and scored on the same criteria. The owner still decides. The difference is that the decision can be explained to a partner, a lender, or the site manager whose project did not make the cut, and it can be revisited when facts change.

## Step one: the constraint check

Before any project is scored, it passes through a constraint check. This is a gate, not a score. It asks whether the project touches something that limits what may be done, regardless of cost or urgency.

- **Recorded approvals.** Does the work affect a zoning condition, a variance, a site plan approval, or a permit?
- **Lease or contract obligations.** Does a lease, service agreement, or warranty say who must do this work, how, or with whose consent?
- **Recorded instruments.** Does the work sit on or near an easement, a servitude, or a shared area another party has rights in?
- **Financing terms.** Does a loan agreement require notice or approval for work of this type or size?
- **Operating dependencies.** Would the work close a location, block access, or interrupt a service others rely on?

Each project gets one of two marks: "clear" or "check first." A "check first" mark does not remove a project from the list. It means the constraint must be read and answered before the project is funded, and that answer may change the scope or the cost. Where the constraint is legal in nature, counsel should read the governing document. The operator's job is to find it and flag it.

## Step two: the scoring rubric

Projects that have been through the gate are scored from 1 to 5 on five criteria. Each criterion carries a weight. The weights below are illustrative. Set your own, write them down, and keep them fixed for the year so scores stay comparable.

| Criterion | Question it answers | Weight (illustrative) |
|---|---|---|
| Safety and legal exposure | What happens to people or obligations if this waits? | 3 |
| Cost of deferral | Does the problem or its price grow while it waits? | 2 |
| Operations or revenue at risk | Would failure stop work, close space, or lose income? | 2 |
| Cost certainty | How confident is the estimate? | 1 |
| Dependency | Do other projects or plans depend on this one? | 1 |

A score of 5 means high: high exposure, high cost of waiting, high certainty. Multiply each score by its weight and add the results. The highest possible total with these weights is 45.

## A worked example

The following example is illustrative. Imagine the dental group with four offices from earlier in this series, with five capital requests on the table and a budget that covers roughly two of them this year.

| Project | Gate | Safety ×3 | Deferral ×2 | Operations ×2 | Certainty ×1 | Dependency ×1 | Total |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| A. Replace sterilizer, Office 2 | Clear | 5 | 4 | 4 | 4 | 2 | 37 |
| B. Rooftop HVAC unit, Office 3 | Check first | 3 | 4 | 4 | 3 | 2 | 30 |
| C. Practice software upgrade, all offices | Clear | 2 | 2 | 3 | 2 | 4 | 22 |
| D. Reseal and restripe lot, Office 1 | Check first | 2 | 3 | 2 | 4 | 1 | 21 |
| E. New exterior sign, Office 4 | Check first | 1 | 1 | 2 | 5 | 1 | 15 |

To read one row: project A scores 5 × 3, plus 4 × 2, plus 4 × 2, plus 4 × 1, plus 2 × 1, which is 15 + 8 + 8 + 4 + 2, for a total of 37.

The ranking puts A and B at the top. B carries a "check first" mark because Office 3 is leased, and the lease may assign responsibility for rooftop equipment to the landlord or the tenant. That question gets answered before the group spends anything. If the lease places the unit with the landlord, B drops off the group's list and its budget moves to C. The lot at Office 1 carries the same mark because restriping can change the space count a site approval depends on. The sign at Office 4 needs a permit check.

The scores order the conversation. They do not end it. An owner may fund C ahead of B for reasons the rubric does not capture, and that is a legitimate choice, as long as the reason is written down beside the ranking.

## The constraint that decides the scope

Some constraints do more than delay a project. They define what the project is allowed to be.

The illustrative owner of six small-bay industrial buildings shows how. A truck court repaving at one building looks like a simple cost item. The constraint check finds that a neighboring owner holds a recorded access right across part of the court, and a tenant's lease guarantees loading access during business hours. The project is still worth doing. But it must be phased, scheduled around the tenant, and coordinated with the neighbor, and those facts change the estimate and the timing. A ranking that skipped the gate would have scored the wrong project.

### The view from Arnould Blvd

On The Blvd Shopping Center operates under a recorded parking variance, Entry 99-11797, that lists 324 spaces provided against 344 required and limits floor space to available parking. The recorded plat's own striping labels total 314. Any parking-lot project at a center under a variance like this one has to protect the space count before it is scored on anything else. A resurfacing and restriping job that looks routine on a cost sheet could, if drawn carelessly, remove stalls the approval already counts as committed, and that would narrow what the center may lease. So a lot project there would carry a "check first" mark by default, with a before-and-after stall count compared against the variance before a contractor starts. The operating detail is covered in "Parking Is the Lease: How a 324/344 Variance Governs Every Deal" in the Shopping Center Operator Series. The lesson for capital ranking is that the cost column is only one of the constraints on the decision.

## Deferral is a decision too

Projects below the funding line are not rejected. They are deferred, and a deferral deserves a record of its own.

- **The reason.** Budget, sequence, a pending constraint answer, or a judgment that it can wait.
- **A revisit date.** Usually the next budget cycle, sooner for anything scored high on cost of deferral.
- **The trigger that moves it up.** A failed inspection, a repeat repair, a tenant or staff complaint, or a quote that expires.
- **The watch item.** What the site manager checks in the meantime, and how often.

A deferred list with dates and triggers is a working document. It feeds the "coming up" section of the owner report and the next year's ranking, so the Office 4 sign and the Office 1 lot do not start from zero next year.

## The practical next step

1. Collect every open capital request across your locations onto one list, one project per row.
2. Run each project through the constraint check and mark it "clear" or "check first."
3. Set your weights for the five criteria, write them down, and score each project.
4. Rank the list, draw the funding line, and write a one-line reason beside any project funded out of rank order.
5. Give every deferred project a revisit date and a trigger that would move it up.
