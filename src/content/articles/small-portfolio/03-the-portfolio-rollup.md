# The Portfolio Rollup: Five Numbers an Owner Should See Every Month

An owner with several sites receives plenty of reports and still has trouble answering the basic question: where should I spend my attention this month? A rollup is a short set of numbers, calculated the same way at every site, that answers that question on one page. This article defines five rollup numbers that work for a building owner and for a multi-location operating business, shows a worked illustrative rollup, and explains how to read it without overreacting.

## What a rollup is for

A **portfolio rollup** is a summary that puts every site on the same row structure so the owner can scan across them. It is not a full financial statement, and it does not replace one. Its job is triage: point the owner to the one or two sites that need a conversation this month.

That job sets three requirements.

- **Few numbers.** More than five or six, and the owner stops reading across and starts reading down.
- **Same definitions everywhere.** A rollup built on local definitions produces a tidy table of numbers that cannot be compared. The previous article, "One Chart of Accounts, Many Locations: Making the Numbers Comparable," covers the groundwork.
- **Each number has an owner.** Someone at each site is responsible for the figure being right, and someone at the portfolio level is responsible for asking about it.

## The five numbers

The five numbers below are chosen because each answers a different question and each has a direct equivalent in a building portfolio and in an operating business. Together they cover money, capacity, commitments, and work.

| # | Number | Question it answers | Building owner version | Multi-location business version |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Collection rate | Is the money we billed coming in? | Rent and recoveries collected ÷ billed for the month | Payments collected ÷ amounts billed to patients and insurers for the month |
| 2 | Site operating result vs. plan | Is each site earning what we expected? | Net operating income (income less operating expenses, before debt service and capital) against budget | Site contribution (revenue less site-level costs) against budget |
| 3 | Capacity in use | How much of what we have is working? | Leased area ÷ leasable area, using one stated denominator | Booked clinical hours ÷ available clinical hours |
| 4 | Commitments due in 180 days | What dated obligations are coming? | Count of lease expirations, renewal options, notice windows, and service contracts due within 180 days | Count of staff license renewals, equipment service dates, vendor contracts, and lease dates due within 180 days |
| 5 | Aged open work | Is work getting finished? | Work orders open longer than the target time for their priority | Maintenance and equipment tickets open longer than target |

Each definition needs a line of explanation.

### 1. Collection rate

Collection rate measures cash behavior, not profitability. A site can show strong income on paper and still have a collection problem. Use the same period and the same rule for partial payments at every site. For the dental group, the rate depends on how insurance timing is handled; pick one convention with your accountant and hold it.

### 2. Site operating result versus plan

**Net operating income**, or NOI, is income minus the costs of running the property, before loan payments and capital projects. For an operating business, the parallel is site contribution. Report it against the site's own plan, not against the other sites. A small building and a large building should not be ranked by raw dollars.

### 3. Capacity in use

For a building owner this is occupancy by area, divided by the one leasable figure in your denominator register. For the dental group it is how full the schedule was. Both tell you whether the asset you are paying for is producing. Both are more useful as a trend than as a single month.

### 4. Commitments due in 180 days

This is a count, not a dollar figure. It tells the owner how much dated work is approaching: expirations, options, notice windows, renewals, and service dates. A rising count is not bad news. It is a workload signal, and a site with twelve commitments in the window needs a different month than a site with two.

### 5. Aged open work

Count the work items that have been open longer than their target. A leaking roof and a burned-out parking light should have different targets. What matters is that the target exists, and that the count of items past it is visible. This number is often the earliest sign that a site's day-to-day operation needs support.

## A worked rollup, illustrative

The table below is illustrative. It shows a single month for the owner of six small-bay industrial buildings introduced earlier in this series. None of these figures describe a real property.

| Building | Collection rate | NOI vs. plan | Leased area | Commitments in 180 days | Aged open work |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 99% | +2% | 100% | 2 | 0 |
| 2 | 97% | −1% | 92% | 4 | 1 |
| 3 | 88% | −9% | 100% | 1 | 0 |
| 4 | 100% | +1% | 83% | 6 | 3 |
| 5 | 98% | 0% | 100% | 3 | 0 |
| 6 | 99% | +3% | 100% | 1 | 1 |

Reading across, two buildings stand out, for different reasons.

Building 3 is fully leased but collecting less than it billed, and its result is behind plan. The first conversation is about one or two tenants, not about the building. Building 4 has a vacancy, a heavy commitment window, and three aged work items. That combination suggests the site needs operating attention: leasing, renewals, and maintenance are all pulling on the same month.

The same reading works for the illustrative dental group with four offices. An office with a strong schedule but a weak collection rate points to billing and insurance follow-up. An office with a thin schedule and several equipment tickets past target points to capacity lost to downtime. The five numbers do not diagnose the cause. They point to where the diagnosis should happen.

## Reading the rollup without overreacting

A rollup invites quick conclusions. A few habits keep it useful.

- **Read three months, not one.** A single month can swing on timing. A pattern across three months is a signal.
- **Set a threshold for each number.** Decide in advance what counts as worth a conversation, for example a collection rate below a level you choose or more than two aged work items. Anything inside the threshold is left alone.
- **Ask before you act.** The rollup tells you which site to call. The site lead tells you why.
- **Keep the notes.** A one-line explanation next to each flagged number becomes the history that makes next month's rollup faster to read.

AI can help assemble and annotate the rollup. It can pull the five numbers from the underlying records, compare each against its threshold, flag the sites that crossed one, and draft a first-pass note for each flag. The owner and the site leads review the flags and decide what happens next. AI does not decide which site receives capital or which tenant receives a notice.

### The view from Arnould Blvd

A rollup is only as trustworthy as the records underneath it. Command Platform, the software that runs On The Blvd Shopping Center, is built on one source of truth: a lease date, a term, or a critical date is entered once, and every sheet that needs it reads from that one record. Change a renewal date in the lease record and it moves to the dates board and the action board without anyone retyping it. That design is what a rollup depends on. A commitments count assembled from three separate lists will disagree with itself sooner or later, and an owner who catches one disagreement stops trusting the whole page. A rollup that reads from a single record can be wrong only in one place, which means it can be corrected in one place.

## Where the numbers come from

Each rollup number should trace to one source, named in writing.

| Number | Source record | Site owner | Portfolio reviewer |
|---|---|---|---|
| Collection rate | Billing and receipts ledger | Bookkeeper or office manager | Owner |
| Operating result vs. plan | Monthly financials on the shared chart | Bookkeeper | Owner and accountant |
| Capacity in use | Rent roll or schedule, with the stated denominator | Property or office manager | Owner |
| Commitments in 180 days | Critical-dates record | Property or office manager | Owner |
| Aged open work | Work order or ticket log | Maintenance lead | Owner |

If a number has no single source, fix that before adding it to the rollup. A number assembled by hand each month will eventually be assembled differently.

## The practical next step

1. Write the five definitions on one page, adapted to your business, and share them with every site lead.
2. Name the source record, the site owner, and the reviewer for each number.
3. Build the first rollup by hand from last month's records, even if it takes an afternoon.
4. Set one threshold per number that would prompt a conversation.
5. Run the rollup for three consecutive months before changing any definition.
