# Shared Vendors, Separate Sites: Managing Services Across a Portfolio

Once a business runs more than one location, the same landscaper, cleaning company, or HVAC contractor often ends up serving several of them. That is usually a sensible choice: one relationship, one set of insurance papers, one invoice format. This article gives you a vendor-site matrix, one row for every vendor at every site, so a shared vendor stays accountable to each place it serves and a problem at one location does not disappear inside a portfolio-wide contract.

## Why shared vendors make sense, and where they drift

Owners consolidate vendors for good reasons. Pricing often improves with volume. There is one account manager to call. The vendor learns how the owner likes the work done. For a small portfolio, a shared vendor can be the difference between having a dependable service partner and having none.

The drift shows up slowly. The contract is signed once, at the portfolio level, and describes the service in general terms. Each site has its own details: a gate code, a loading dock that closes at noon, a tenant or staff member who needs notice before a crew arrives. Those details live with whoever manages the site, and the vendor's crew learns them by trial. When a site manager or a crew lead changes, the knowledge leaves with them.

Consider the illustrative owner of six small-bay industrial buildings from earlier in this series. One landscaping contractor maintains all six. Two buildings share a detention pond that needs mowing on its own schedule. One building's tenants run early shifts and object when blowers start at seven. The contract says "grounds maintenance, all locations, weekly." Everything that makes the work go well at each building sits outside that sentence.

Now take the illustrative dental group with four offices. One company provides after-hours cleaning at all four, and one equipment technician services the sterilizers. The cleaning crew needs to know which rooms hold equipment it must not touch. The technician needs each office's service history, not the group's. Same vendors, four different jobs.

## The vendor-site matrix

The fix is a single table where the unit of record is the vendor-site pair, not the vendor. Each row answers the questions a crew, a site manager, and an owner would each ask.

| Column | What it records | Example entry (illustrative) |
|---|---|---|
| Vendor | Company and service line | Grounds contractor, mowing and beds |
| Site | One location per row | Building 4 |
| Scope at this site | What is included here, in plain terms | Weekly mow, beds monthly, pond edge every other week |
| Site rules | Access, hours, notice, hazards | No equipment before 8 a.m.; gate code held by site lead |
| Site contact | The person the crew calls on the day | Building 4 lead, with a named backup |
| Approver | Who authorizes extra work at this site | Operations manager, up to a stated limit |
| Where work is recorded | The work-order list for this site | Building 4 work orders, not a shared inbox |
| Paper on file | Agreement, insurance, site scope sheet | Master agreement plus Building 4 scope sheet |
| Last confirmed | Date the row was checked with the vendor | A date, not "recently" |

Three rules keep the matrix useful.

- **One row per vendor per site.** A vendor serving six buildings has six rows. The repetition is deliberate, because each row can differ.
- **Site rules come from the site.** The person who manages the location writes that column, and the vendor confirms it.
- **Every row names where work is recorded.** If the honest answer is "the vendor emails the owner," the row has a gap worth closing.

The matrix can live in a spreadsheet. What matters is that it exists in one place and that each site manager can find their own rows without reading everyone else's.

## Shared terms once, site facts per site

A master service agreement typically covers the terms that should be the same everywhere: insurance, payment, termination, standards of work. The facts that differ belong in a short scope sheet for each location. That splits the paper along the same line as the matrix. Shared terms are written once. Site facts are written per site, by the people who know them. The master agreement is a contract, and counsel should read it. The scope sheets are operating documents a site manager can keep current.

Pricing follows the same split. Ask for pricing by site, even when the vendor invoices the portfolio on one bill. A single portfolio number hides which location costs what, and it makes the per-site comparison described in "One Chart of Accounts, Many Locations: Making the Numbers Comparable" impossible for that line of expense.

Extra work follows it too. A request for additional service at one site is approved by that site's approver, recorded against that site, and priced on its own line. If the cleaning company strips and refinishes floors at one dental office, the invoice should say which office, not "additional services, all locations." A change order that cannot be traced to a site cannot be checked by the person who watched the work happen.

## Who sees what

A shared vendor raises a question single-site operations rarely face: what should the vendor be able to see? A company serving several sites does not need the owner's full file for any of them. It does not need other sites' tenant or patient details, other vendors' pricing, or the owner's financial records.

The answer is a scoped view. Each party sees its own slice of the records and nothing else. For a vendor, the slice is its own work orders, at the sites it serves, with the site rules and contacts it needs to do them. That scope does three jobs at once.

- **The crew finds the work.** Open items, site rules, and the day's contact sit in one place, so the crew is not piecing together a job from texts and forwarded emails.
- **The owner limits exposure.** Information unrelated to the job never reaches the vendor, which narrows what can be misplaced or misused.
- **Completion is recorded where the work was assigned.** When the vendor closes an item, the site manager and the owner see it on the same record, at the same site.

This works on paper as well as in software. A shared folder per vendor, holding only that vendor's scope sheets and work-order lists, applies the same rule.

### The view from Arnould Blvd

At On The Blvd Shopping Center, Command Platform gives each vendor a scoped view: a vendor sees its own work orders and nothing else in the file. The platform was built around this one real center, and the rule was written for this center's work. The rule itself does not depend on how many sites there are. Applied in any portfolio, the same scoping is what lets one vendor serve several sites without seeing all of them, because the vendor's view is a filter on the work assigned to it rather than a copy of any site's records. In a portfolio built that way, adding a location adds rows to a vendor's list. It does not open a door into anyone else's file.

## Reviewing shared vendors site by site

A shared vendor is reviewed at two levels: the relationship as a whole, and the work at each site.

Once a quarter, walk the matrix with each shared vendor. Confirm every row, update site rules and contacts, and date the "last confirmed" column. The meeting takes minutes per site and catches the gate code that changed, the backup contact who left, and the scope item nobody has done since spring.

Once a year, before renewal, compare the work by site. Look at the work orders opened and closed at each location, the repeat calls, and the complaints. Suppose, as an illustration, that the dental group's HVAC contractor closes items promptly at three offices and slowly at the fourth. The matrix usually shows why. The fourth office's site contact may be blank, its approver may be the owner who is rarely on site, or its scope sheet may never have been written. The question to settle is whether the issue sits with the vendor, the site, or the handoff between them.

Sometimes the answer is that one site needs a different vendor: a building with different equipment, a location outside the vendor's usual area, a tenant with requirements the vendor cannot meet. The matrix lets the owner make that change for one row without reopening the whole relationship.

## The practical next step

1. List every vendor that serves more than one of your locations, and note the service line for each.
2. Build the vendor-site matrix with one row per vendor-site pair, and ask each site manager to fill in site rules and the site contact.
3. Walk the rows with each shared vendor, correct them, and date every row.
4. At the next invoice or renewal, ask each shared vendor for pricing broken out by site.
5. Check what each vendor can see today in your shared folders or software, and narrow it to its own work at its own sites.
