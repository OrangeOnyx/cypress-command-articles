# One Chart of Accounts, Many Locations: Making the Numbers Comparable

An owner with several locations wants to know which one is doing well and which one needs attention. The numbers exist, but each site has recorded them its own way, so the comparison says more about bookkeeping habits than about performance. This article gives you two tools: an account mapping worksheet that brings local codes onto one shared chart, and a denominator register that fixes what every per-unit figure is divided by.

## Why comparable numbers come first

A **chart of accounts** is the list of categories a business uses to record money coming in and going out: rent income, repairs, utilities, supplies, payroll, and so on. Each site of a small portfolio usually starts with the chart it inherited, from a previous owner, a bookkeeper's template, or a software default.

That works while each site is read on its own. It stops working the moment the owner puts two sites side by side. If one site books a roof patch as a repair and another books a similar patch as a capital improvement, their repair lines cannot be compared. If one office records lab fees as supplies and another records them as a cost of services, their supply lines cannot be compared.

The problem is not accuracy. Each site may be perfectly accurate by its own rules. The problem is that the rules differ, and a rollup built on different rules produces confident numbers that mean different things.

## The two running examples

The illustrative owner of six small-bay industrial buildings, introduced in the first article of this series, bought her buildings over several years. Two came with a previous owner's bookkeeping. One building's records split common-area electricity into its own account; the other five fold it into general utilities. Two buildings record tenant reimbursements as income; the rest net them against the expense.

The illustrative dental group with four offices has a similar story. The acquired office kept its prior chart. One office codes a dental equipment service call to repairs, another to equipment, a third to a general "office expense" line. Each office manager is consistent within the office. None is consistent with the others.

In both cases, the owner's first instinct is to ask the accountant for a cleaner report. A better first step is to agree on the definitions the report will use.

## The account mapping worksheet

The mapping worksheet is the core tool of this article. It lists every account each site uses today and assigns it to one shared account. Nothing is deleted. Local detail can survive as a sub-account, but every local code rolls up to a common line.

Here is an illustrative excerpt for the industrial owner.

| Local account | Site | Shared account | Rule for new entries | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| CAM Electric | Building 2 | Utilities: common area | All common-area power | Keep as sub-account |
| Utilities | Buildings 1, 3–6 | Utilities: common area | Split tenant-metered from common | Requires meter list |
| Roof Repair | Building 4 | Repairs: roof | Repairs under the capital threshold | Threshold set by owner and accountant |
| Roof Improvements | Building 1 | Capital: roof | Replacement or new system only | Review two prior entries |
| Tenant Reimb. Income | Buildings 2, 5 | Recoveries | Record gross, not netted | Other sites restate going forward |

And for the dental group.

| Local account | Office | Shared account | Rule for new entries | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Lab Fees | Offices A, C | Cost of services: lab | All outside lab charges | |
| Clinical Supplies | Office B | Cost of services: lab / Supplies: clinical | Split by vendor | Office B mixes both today |
| Equipment Service | Office A | Repairs: clinical equipment | Service calls and parts | |
| Office Expense | Office D | Several | Reassign by vendor | Largest cleanup item |

Four columns do the work.

- **Shared account** is the line every site reports to.
- **Rule for new entries** is a one-sentence definition someone can apply without asking. "Repairs under the capital threshold" is a rule. "Roof stuff" is not.
- **Notes** capture the history you need to explain a restated number.
- **Site** tells you who needs to change a habit, and who does not.

Keep the shared chart short. A small portfolio rarely needs more than a few dozen operating accounts. Every extra account is another place for two sites to disagree.

Decisions about capital thresholds, how recoveries are recorded, and how prior periods are restated belong to the owner and the accountant together. The worksheet records the decision. It does not make it.

## The denominator register

Account definitions fix the numerator. Per-unit comparisons also need a fixed denominator, and that is where many portfolios quietly drift.

A per-square-foot operating cost depends entirely on which square feet you divide by. Gross building area, rentable area, and leasable area are different numbers. A building with a mezzanine may count it in one measure and not another. For a dental office, "per chair," "per operatory," "per provider-day," and "per patient visit" each tell a different story, and each is legitimate if everyone uses the same one.

The denominator register is a one-page list of every unit the portfolio divides by, with four entries for each.

| Denominator | Definition | Source document | Value by site |
|---|---|---|---|
| Leasable SF | Area available for lease, measured to the standard the owner adopts | Measurement report or recorded plan | One stated figure per building |
| Operatory | A treatment room equipped for patient care | Office floor plan and equipment list | One stated count per office |
| Provider-day | One provider scheduled for one clinical day | Scheduling system | Monthly count per office |

Two rules keep the register useful. First, each site has exactly one stated value for each denominator, with its source. Second, when a value changes, the change is recorded with a date, so last year's per-unit figures can be read against the value that applied then.

An illustrative example shows why this matters. Suppose one industrial building reports its operating cost per square foot using a gross area figure, and its neighbor uses a leasable area figure that excludes a shared corridor. The two buildings could have identical costs per leasable foot and still appear different in the rollup. Nobody made an error. The comparison was set up with two denominators.

## Comparable enough for the people who read it

The numbers do not need to be perfect to be useful. They need to be consistent and explained. When a lender, partner, or buyer reads a portfolio summary, they typically look first for whether the figures across sites were prepared the same way. Terms and expectations vary by lender and by market, but a clear statement of definitions tends to shorten the conversation, and an unexplained inconsistency tends to lengthen it.

AI can help once the worksheet exists. It can compare a month's entries against the mapping rules and flag items that look miscoded, extract vendor names and amounts from invoices to suggest a shared account, and prepare a first-pass variance note for each site. A bookkeeper or the owner reviews the flags and makes the entry. AI does not decide what counts as capital, and it does not restate prior periods on its own.

### The view from Arnould Blvd

On The Blvd Shopping Center is usually described as a center of approximately 63,000 square feet. Its audit-grade gross leasable area is 62,883 square feet. The difference is 117 square feet, small in conversation and real on paper. "Approximately 63,000" is the right figure for describing the property. 62,883 is the right figure for dividing by. A per-square-foot number built on one and compared against a number built on the other would carry a small error that nobody could see. The fix is the denominator register: one stated figure, its source, and a rule that every per-square-foot calculation uses it. In a portfolio of several sites, those small differences stack, and the register is what keeps them from stacking.

## Keeping the shared chart from drifting

A shared chart is a habit as much as a document. A few practices help it hold.

- **Name an owner for the chart.** One person approves new accounts. Sites request; the owner decides.
- **Review miscodes monthly.** A short list of entries that did not follow the rules, sent back to the site, trains the habit faster than a policy memo.
- **Onboard new sites to the chart, not the other way around.** When the industrial owner buys a seventh building, its records are mapped on the first day. When the dental group opens a fifth office, the office manager starts with the shared chart.
- **Restate once, then move on.** Decide how far back to restate with your accountant, do it once, and note the date in the register.

## The practical next step

1. Export the chart of accounts from each site and list every account in one worksheet with a column for the site.
2. Draft a short shared chart, then map every local account to it with a one-sentence rule for new entries.
3. Build a denominator register: list every unit you divide by, define it, and record one stated value per site with its source.
4. Review the mapping and the capital threshold with your accountant before applying it to any prior period.
5. Name one person who approves new accounts, and schedule a monthly miscode review.
