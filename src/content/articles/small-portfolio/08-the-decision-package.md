# The Decision Package: What a Lender, Partner, or Buyer Should Be Able to See

Sooner or later someone outside the business asks to see it whole: a lender considering a loan, a prospective partner, a buyer, an appraiser, an insurer. Many owners assemble the answer under deadline, pulling files from inboxes and drives and explaining the gaps in person. This article gives you a decision package: a standing, dated set of records organized so an outside reader can follow the business without the owner narrating, including a schedule of what the package does not cover. It closes the series by pulling the earlier tools into one place.

## What a decision package is

A decision package is the answer to the question "show me the business" prepared before anyone asks. It is built from the same records the business already runs on, organized in a fixed order, dated, and kept current on a schedule.

The purpose is readiness, not a transaction. Most owners who keep one never use it for a sale. They use it for a refinance, a new partner, an insurance renewal, a family succession conversation, or simply their own annual review. The package earns its keep every time someone needs to see the whole picture quickly and accurately.

Consider, as an illustration, a family-owned restaurant group with three locations whose founder wants to bring a longtime manager in as a minority partner. The manager's advisor asks for statements by location, the leases, the equipment financing, and a list of known issues. A group with a standing package sends a dated index the same week. A group without one spends a month rebuilding records it already had, and the conversation cools while it waits.

What each outside reader asks for varies with the reader, the market, and the transaction. Lenders typically look for evidence that income is stable and documented, and many calculate a debt service coverage ratio, or DSCR: the property's or business's net operating income divided by its annual loan payments. Partners typically want to understand how decisions are made and who carries which obligations. Buyers typically want to test every number against its source. The package does not try to predict every request. It gives each reader a reliable starting point and a clear path from each summary figure back to the document behind it.

## The contents

The package has nine sections. The order moves from the overview to the detail, and most sections draw on a tool from earlier in this series.

| # | Section | What it contains | Draws on |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Summary and scope | What the package covers, as-of date, who maintains it | This article |
| 2 | Locations | Legal descriptions, site plans, zoning, area, entity that owns each | Recorded documents and property files |
| 3 | Revenue records | Rent roll or revenue by location, reconciled to source documents | "One Chart of Accounts, Many Locations" |
| 4 | Operating statements | Statements by location on one chart of accounts, with a portfolio total | "One Chart of Accounts, Many Locations" |
| 5 | Monthly reporting | The last twelve owner reports and the rollup history | "The Portfolio Rollup" and "The Monthly Owner Report" |
| 6 | Contracts and obligations | Leases, service agreements, recorded instruments, critical dates | "Shared Vendors, Separate Sites" |
| 7 | Capital history and plan | Completed projects, the current ranked list, deferred items with triggers | "Ranking Capital Needs You Can't All Fund This Year" |
| 8 | Open items | Known issues, pending questions, documents not yet obtained | This article |
| 9 | Exclusions | What the package does not cover, and why | This article |

Two principles govern every section. First, each figure points to its record. A total in section 4 should trace to the statements, and a rent figure in section 3 should trace to a lease or an invoice. Second, the package uses the same denominators and definitions as the monthly reporting. If area, revenue, or expense categories are defined one way in the owner report and another in the package, the first careful reader will find the difference.

## The exclusions schedule

Section 9 is the one most packages leave out, and it is often the one that protects the owner most. An exclusions schedule states plainly what the reader might assume is included but is not.

Consider the illustrative dental group with four offices. The building that houses one office is owned by the founding dentist personally and leased to the practice. The practice's package includes the lease. It does not include the building, and the exclusions schedule says so in one line. Without that line, a reader looking at the site list could reasonably assume the practice owns real estate it does not.

Now the illustrative owner of six small-bay industrial buildings. One of the six sits in a separate company with its own lender. The package covers five buildings in full and lists the sixth in the exclusions schedule, with a note on where its records live. A lender reviewing the five is not left to discover the sixth in a footnote.

Common entries on an exclusions schedule include:

- **Parcels sold or never owned**, even where they sit inside the property's outline.
- **Assets held by a different entity**, including related parties.
- **Leased equipment**, which belongs to the lessor.
- **Tenant or customer property** located on site.
- **Rights that depend on a neighbor**, such as shared parking or access, with the instrument and its end date.

A package that silently includes something the business does not own creates a finding in the reader's first review. A package that states the exclusion up front shows that the owner knows the edges of the business.

### The view from Arnould Blvd

The goal at On The Blvd Shopping Center is a property whose records can be handed to a lender or buyer intact. The package for a center like this one would state that it is zoned CH and sits on 4.84 acres, with the site plan drawn from the recorded plat. It would also show what is not included. The corner bank parcel was sold and is not part of the center. The plat marks it "NOT A PART," and the site plan draws it as a notch in the center's outline rather than as center property. That one entry on the exclusions schedule prevents the most common drawing error a marketing plan can make, and it tells an outside reader that the boundary was checked against the record. The same discipline applies in any business: the package describes what is owned, and it names what is not.

## Keep it current, not assembled

A package assembled in a hurry is a snapshot taken under pressure. A package kept current is a byproduct of ordinary operations. Three habits make the difference.

- **One owner.** A named person maintains the package and dates each version.
- **A schedule.** Sections 3 through 5 refresh monthly from the records that feed the owner report. Sections 2, 6, and 7 are reviewed each quarter. The exclusions schedule is reviewed whenever an entity, a parcel, or a shared right changes.
- **An open-items log.** Anything missing, pending, or uncertain goes into section 8 with a name and a date, so a reader sees the gap stated rather than discovers it.

AI can help with the upkeep. It can compare the package against its index and flag missing documents, extract dates and terms from new contracts into the obligations section, and draft a summary for a specific reader. A person reviews every draft, decides what is shared and with whom, and signs any cover letter. What leaves the business is the owner's decision.

## The practical next step

1. Create a folder with the nine section headings and a one-page index that dates the current version.
2. Fill sections 2 through 5 from the records you already keep, and mark each figure with the record it comes from.
3. Write the exclusions schedule: every parcel, entity, asset, or shared right a reader might assume is included but is not.
4. Start the open-items log and name an owner for each item.
5. Set the refresh schedule, monthly and quarterly, and put the first quarterly review on the calendar.
