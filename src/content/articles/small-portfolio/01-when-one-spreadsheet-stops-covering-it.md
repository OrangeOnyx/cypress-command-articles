# When One Spreadsheet Stops Covering It: Signs a Portfolio Needs a System

Most small portfolios run for years on one good spreadsheet and one person who knows what it means. That arrangement works until the questions outgrow the file. This article gives you a ten-signal readiness scorecard for deciding whether your spreadsheet is still enough, and it introduces the two illustrative businesses this series follows from here to the decision package in the final article.

## Two businesses, one question

This series follows two illustrative examples. Neither is a client. Both are composites built to show how the same operating question plays out in different kinds of owner-led businesses.

The first is **an owner of six small-bay industrial buildings**. The buildings sit in two nearby towns. Each has between six and twelve bays leased to contractors, distributors, and light manufacturers. The owner has a part-time bookkeeper, a leasing broker on call, and a maintenance vendor list that lives in her phone.

The second is **a dental group with four offices**. The founding dentist opened the first office, added a second through an acquisition, and opened two more over the following years. Each office has its own office manager, its own schedule, and its own habits for ordering supplies and coding expenses.

These two businesses look different. One collects rent. The other bills patients and insurers. What they share is the moment this article is about: the point where the owner asks a simple question, such as "which location needs my attention this month?", and the honest answer is "let me pull a few things together and get back to you."

## What the spreadsheet does well

Before looking for its limits, give the spreadsheet its due. For a single site, or for two sites run by the same person, a well-kept spreadsheet is often the right tool.

- **It is fast to change.** A new column takes seconds. No vendor, no configuration, no training.
- **It matches how the owner thinks.** The person who built it knows every tab and every shortcut.
- **It is cheap.** The cost is time, and at small scale the time is modest.
- **It is portable.** It can be emailed to a lender, a partner, or an accountant without an export step.

None of that disappears as the portfolio grows. What changes is the load. Each new site adds rows, tabs, and exceptions. Each new person who touches the file adds a second way of doing things. The spreadsheet does not fail all at once. It gets a little slower to trust every month.

## The ten signals

The scorecard below is the tool for this article. Score each signal honestly: 0 if it does not apply, 1 if it happens occasionally, 2 if it happens every month. Fill it in with the people who actually use the file, not from memory.

| # | Signal | Industrial owner example | Dental group example |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | The same fact lives in more than one place | Lease expiration in the rent tab and in a separate renewals list | Provider hours in the schedule and in the payroll sheet |
| 2 | Numbers from different sites cannot be compared | One building reports gross area, another rentable area | Two offices code lab fees to different accounts |
| 3 | One person is the only reliable reader | Only the owner knows which bays are on month-to-month terms | Only the founder knows which offices share a hygienist |
| 4 | Dates are tracked by memory or email | Renewal notices found by searching the inbox | Equipment service dates on sticky notes |
| 5 | Month-end takes longer each month | The bookkeeper reconciles six tabs by hand | Each office manager sends a different report format |
| 6 | Versions disagree | "Rent Roll FINAL v3" and "Rent Roll FINAL v4" both in use | The owner and the accountant have different totals |
| 7 | Outside parties see too much or too little | The broker receives the full file to find two vacancies | A new office manager is given access to every office |
| 8 | Work orders and money live apart | Roof repairs tracked in texts, costs in the ledger | Equipment repairs in email, invoices in accounting |
| 9 | A lender or partner request takes days | Assembling a package means rebuilding the history | A partner asks for per-office results and waits a week |
| 10 | A new site would mean a new way of doing things | The seventh building would get its own tab and its own habits | A fifth office would copy whichever office it was near |

Read the totals as a starting point, not a verdict.

- **0 to 6:** The spreadsheet is probably still the right tool. Tighten it: one owner per tab, one date format, one version.
- **7 to 13:** The spreadsheet is carrying more than it was built for. Standardize before adding software. The next three articles in this series cover the standards that matter most.
- **14 to 20:** The business is asking questions a single file cannot answer reliably. A system, whether purpose-built or configured from existing software, is worth scoping.

The scores matter less than the pattern. Signals 1, 4, and 6 together point to a source-of-truth problem. Signals 2 and 5 point to a comparability problem. Signals 3, 7, and 9 point to an access and handoff problem. Each pattern calls for a different first move.

## Reading the pattern for each example

Apply the scorecard to the two illustrative businesses.

The industrial owner scores high on signals 1, 4, and 8. Her lease dates live in two places, notices surface through inbox searches, and repair work is disconnected from repair costs. Her comparability score is lower, because all six buildings are the same product type and she measures them the same way. Her first move is a single dated record for every lease and every service contract, with the renewal list generated from it rather than kept beside it.

The dental group scores high on signals 2, 5, and 10. Each office codes expenses its own way, month-end is a translation exercise, and each new office would inherit a local habit. Its first move is a shared chart of accounts and a single month-end format, covered in the next article, "One Chart of Accounts, Many Locations: Making the Numbers Comparable."

Same scorecard, different first moves. That is the point of scoring before buying anything.

## What "a system" actually means here

The word "system" can sound like a software purchase. In this series it means something narrower and more useful: a set of records where each fact lives once, a set of standard questions every site answers the same way, and a set of views that give each person the slice they need.

Software can hold that structure. A disciplined set of linked spreadsheets can hold some of it. What matters is the structure, not the product. An owner who buys software without the structure will usually rebuild the old spreadsheet inside the new tool.

AI can help at the edges once the structure exists. It can summarize a month's activity by site, flag a date that is approaching, extract terms from a new lease, or draft a first-pass owner report for review. It cannot decide which site needs capital or approve a renewal. Those calls stay with the owner.

### The view from Arnould Blvd

On The Blvd Shopping Center is a legacy multi-tenant center at 101–149 Arnould Blvd in Lafayette, Louisiana. It is one property, and Command Platform, the software that runs it, was built around that one real center. The platform is organized as numbered sheets, each answering one operating question. The sheet set grew from thirteen to eighteen as the work asked more of it, and one of the additions is a portfolio rollup sheet. Today that rollup summarizes a single center. It exists because the rollup is a way of asking questions, not a count of buildings: what came in, what is committed, what is coming due, what is open. A single property with two buildings and many units already asks those questions every month. The lesson for this series is that the signal to add structure is the number of questions the work asks, and one real property can ask plenty.

## When to wait

Not every high score calls for new software this quarter. Wait when:

- **The owner cannot yet say what questions the system must answer.** Write the questions first.
- **The data has not been reconciled.** Moving unreconciled records into a new tool moves the disagreements with them.
- **No one owns the change.** A system without a named process owner will drift back to the spreadsheet within months.

Waiting is a decision, not a delay. Record it, set a date to rescore, and use the time to clean the records.

## The practical next step

1. Print the ten-signal scorecard and fill it in this week with the two or three people who use the file most.
2. Circle the signals that score 2 and group them: source of truth, comparability, or access and handoff.
3. Write down the five questions you most often ask about your sites and cannot answer without assembling something.
4. Pick one fact that lives in more than one place, such as a lease expiration or an equipment service date, and make one record the source for it.
5. Put a date on the calendar, about ninety days out, to rescore and compare.
