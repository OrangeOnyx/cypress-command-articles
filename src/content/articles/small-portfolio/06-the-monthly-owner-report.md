# The Monthly Owner Report: One Page That Scales from Two Sites to Ten

Most owner reports start as a useful summary and grow into a packet. Each new location adds pages, each question from last month adds a schedule, and the owner stops reading past the first sheet. This article gives you a one-page report template with seven fixed sections, a rule for how it grows as locations are added, and a way to build it from records the business already keeps, so the report is a view rather than a document someone retypes every month.

## What the report is for

The monthly owner report has one job: tell the owner, in about ten minutes of reading, what needs attention and what needs a decision. It is not a record of everything that happened. The records hold that. The report points into them.

That purpose sets three tests for anything that appears on the page.

- **Would the owner act differently if they knew this?** If not, it belongs in the records, not the report.
- **Is it comparable across sites?** A figure that means something different at each location cannot sit in a shared grid.
- **Can someone trace it to its source?** Every number on the page should lead back to the record it came from, without a phone call.

The last test matters most. When a report is built by copying figures from several systems into a document, it is only as good as the copying. The number is correct on the day it is typed and wrong the day a record changes. A report that reads directly from the records carries their corrections with it.

## The one-page layout

The page has seven sections, always in the same order. The order is part of the design. An owner who reads the report every month learns where to look.

| # | Section | What it contains |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Header | Period, as-of date, who prepared it, and which records it draws from |
| 2 | Portfolio summary | The rollup numbers, with prior month and same month last year |
| 3 | Site grid | One row per location, the same columns for every row, flags on exceptions |
| 4 | Exceptions | Each flagged item: what happened, why, who owns it, next date |
| 5 | Decisions requested | Each decision the owner needs to make, with a recommendation and a deadline |
| 6 | Coming up | Dated obligations in the next ninety days: renewals, notices, inspections, payments |
| 7 | Data notes | What is estimated, what arrived late, what changed since last month |

Section 2 is where the numbers from "The Portfolio Rollup: Five Numbers an Owner Should See Every Month" belong. Put them at the top, in the same order every month, with enough history beside each to show direction. Section 3 then repeats the rollup's measures for each site, so the owner can see which location is moving the portfolio figure.

Sections 5 and 6 are the ones owners tend to value most once they exist. A decision that is written down with a deadline gets made. A date that appears ninety days ahead gets handled.

## Scaling from two sites to ten

The template scales because only one section grows with the number of locations. The site grid gains a row for each site. Everything else stays the same size.

Take the illustrative dental group with four offices. The site grid has four rows. Exceptions are flagged by thresholds the owner sets in advance, so the report surfaces the office that crossed a line rather than narrating all four. If the group opens a fifth office, the grid gets a fifth row, and the rest of the page is unchanged.

The illustrative owner of six small-bay industrial buildings uses the same template with six rows. Two sites or ten, the page holds, because the exceptions section is driven by thresholds rather than by the number of sites. A quiet month at ten sites produces a short exceptions list. A busy month at two can produce a long one. Either way, the owner reads what crossed a line.

A few habits keep the grid readable as it grows.

- **Same columns for every site.** If a measure does not apply to a location, write "n/a" instead of dropping the column.
- **Flags, not colors alone.** A flag with a one-word reason ("late," "over," "open") survives printing and photocopying.
- **Thresholds written down.** Record each threshold in the data notes once a quarter, so a flag means the same thing in March as in June.

Consider, as a further illustration, a family-owned restaurant group with three locations. Its grid needs the same discipline: identical columns, stated thresholds, and one row per location. The measures differ from a dental group's, but the page does not.

## Writing exceptions and decisions

Exceptions are where reports most often drift into narrative. A useful exception entry has five parts and fits in two or three lines.

1. **What happened**, in one sentence, with the figure.
2. **Why**, as far as it is known, with "unknown" written plainly if that is the truth.
3. **What is being done**, and by whom.
4. **When it will be reported again.**
5. **Where the record is**, so the owner can look without asking.

An illustrative entry for the industrial owner might read: "Building 2 receivables past thirty days rose above the stated threshold. One tenant paid late after a change in its accounts-payable staff. The property manager spoke with the tenant on the 12th; payment is promised by the 25th. Next report: mid-month update. Record: Building 2 tenant ledger."

Decisions follow a similar pattern: the question, the options considered, the recommendation, the cost of waiting, and the date by which the owner needs to answer. A decision with no deadline tends to reappear every month.

AI can help prepare this section. It can summarize the month's activity from the records, compare each site against its thresholds, flag the items that crossed a line, and draft the first version of each exception entry. A person who knows the sites reviews every entry before the report goes to the owner, corrects what the draft got wrong, and signs off on the recommendations. The draft saves time. The review keeps the report accountable.

## Who sees which version

In a small portfolio, several people may read a version of the report: the owner, a partner, site managers, perhaps an outside bookkeeper. Each needs a different slice. A site manager needs their own row, their own exceptions, and their own dates. A partner may need the summary and the decisions. The owner needs the whole page.

The temptation is to produce several documents. That multiplies the copying and the chances for two versions to disagree. The better approach is one set of records and several scoped views of it. Each reader sees the portion that belongs to them, drawn from the same source, on the same as-of date.

In the illustrative dental group, each office manager would see their office's row, its exceptions, and its upcoming dates. The group's partners would see the portfolio summary, the site grid, and the decisions requested. The owner would see all seven sections. Nobody assembles three versions. When a figure is corrected at one office, every reader who can see that figure sees the corrected one the next time they look.

## The practical next step

1. Copy the seven-section layout onto one page and fill it in for last month using the records you already keep.
2. Write down the threshold for each measure in the site grid, and record them in the data notes.
3. List every decision you are waiting to make, and give each a recommendation and a deadline in section 5.
4. Identify every figure that someone copies by hand into the report, and note the record it should read from instead.
5. Decide who receives which slice of the report, and replace separate versions with scoped views of one source.
