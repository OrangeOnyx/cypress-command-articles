# When a Single-Asset LLC Should Pay Itself by Hand

Automatic payout is the processor moving cleared money to a linked bank account on its own clock. It is a good factory setting when a bookkeeper watches a dedicated revenue account every day. For a single-asset limited liability company that owns one shopping center, the same setting can move the wrong dollars, to the wrong account, before anyone has matched them to the rent roll.

This article is that case. One property, one company, no treasury desk. When the sweep is the wrong tool, what a weekly manual release looks like, and when to turn automatic payout back on. It is an operating routine. It is not tax advice, not any real company's bank, and not a dashboard tour. No account numbers belong in it.

"Single-asset" is a shape, not a slight. The company holds one center, its leases, and its debt. What it usually does not have is a second person whose only job is to catch a Tuesday sweep that included a tenant deposit and an unmatched receipt.

## What the automatic sweep is deciding for you

Left on, payout answers three questions the owner may not have meant to delegate. When: the processor's schedule, not the morning you reconcile. How much: whatever it calls available, which can include an unidentified receipt, a draft still inside an ACH return window, or a deposit you meant to segregate. Where: the one linked account. If that account pays the lender, the landscaper, and distributions, everything lands in the same pool.

On a busy account with clean separation upstream, those are the right answers. On a company that runs rent, a deposit, and the occasional reimbursement through one pipe, they are decisions. The feature works. The routine that should look at the result was never set. "Why a Good Workflow Fails When the Operating Routine Is Unclear" is this kind of fade.

The cost of a bad sweep is a commingled balance. You can usually move the money back. You will do it after the operating account has already spent cash you were not free to spend. Manual payout is the look before the cash changes rooms.

## Four cases where the sweep is the wrong default

Turn automatic payout off, or do not turn it on, when one of these is true. You do not need all four.

**The linked account is not the account the loan names.** Some loans want rent in a named account, with the lender's controls around it. A payout to whatever account the processor setup asked for can fight that instruction. Read the loan file with counsel and your lender before you link anything. This article cannot say what your loan requires. It can say not to let a settings page overrule the file.

**Cleared is not the same as kept.** ACH receipts can return after they appear. A sweep that empties the processor every afternoon can put Thursday's return into cash you already spent. "ACH or Card: How Multi-Tenant Rent Actually Clears" sets the moment a draft becomes collected. The payout should not run ahead of it. Leave a cushion equal to a few days of drafts, or release only what has passed your return window. Write the number on the routine card.

**More than rent shares the pipe.** A deposit, an overpayment, an unclassified catch-up: an automatic sweep dumps them into operating cash. Deposit cash is a liability, as "Security Deposits: Hold, Apply, and Refund Without Spending the Cash" describes. A sweep does not read your chart of accounts. If you cannot split by type before the payout, do not pay out automatically.

**The distribution is one transfer away.** In a single-asset company the operating account is often one move from the member. That is fine as a decision and a poor accident. Release rent, fund the reserve and the debt service, and only then decide what leaves. Someone stands at that door.

If none of the four is true, automatic payout may be the right setting. Turn it off because a specific dollar would otherwise move early or land in the wrong place, not because manual feels more serious.

## A release routine for one company

Make it a weekly card, finished in the same sitting as the rent register. One person owns it. A backup covers leave. Five lines:

1. **Match.** Every receipt since the last release is on the per-unit register. Anything unmatched stays in the processor.
2. **Age.** Receipts still inside the ACH return window stay behind, or the cushion you defined stays behind.
3. **Split.** Name the portions: operating rent, deposit cash, anything else your accountant specified. If you cannot name it, you cannot release it.
4. **Release.** You move those amounts yourself. Write the date and the amounts on the card. No bank identifiers, no processor account identifiers, no last digits. The processor receipt is the evidence. The card is the decision.
5. **After.** Debt service and the reserve transfer run on their own dates, against cash you actually released. A distribution is a separate line naming who approved it, not the remainder of a sweep.

The card belongs on the calendar next to collections. The person who may release is the process owner, even if a bookkeeper prepares the match. Preparation and release are different acts.

AI can list receipts with no unit, amounts that do not equal the register, and deposits sitting in the operating pile. It can draft the five lines from the export. It cannot approve a release, and it should not have a way to move the money.

Keep a one-line log: date, amount to operating, amount to deposits, amount left behind, and why. A few months of lines will show whether the cushion is right, and they show a later reader that the cash was looked at.

An illustrative Wednesday, not any real company: the processor shows $18,400 available. Of that, $1,200 is a wire with no unit on the register, $4,800 is rent still inside the return window, and $3,000 is a new deposit that arrived through the same pipe. Releasable operating cash is $18,400 minus $1,200 minus $4,800 minus $3,000, which is $9,400. You release $9,400 to the operating account and $3,000 to the deposit account. You leave $6,000 where it is. The log line is those three amounts and the words "unmatched wire, return window, deposit." An empty subtraction is allowed. A release of the whole $18,400 because it was "available" is the mistake the card exists to prevent.

## What "available" is allowed to mean

Processors use "available" for the balance their rules will let you pay out. Your word is stricter. Releasable cash is that balance, minus unmatched items, minus the return-window cushion, minus deposits and other restricted receipts. The remainder is what step 4 may move. If it is zero, you release nothing. An empty week means the filter worked.

Write that subtraction on the card in words. Fees the processor already netted are an expense from its statement, not something to gross-up in your head. The rent register and the bank still have to meet.

## When to turn automatic payout back on

Turn automatic payout back on when all of these are true, and write the date.

- Deposits and odd receipts no longer share the rent balance.
- The linked account is the one your loan file and your accountant agree on.
- The return window is handled by a cushion or by a sweep that does not run early.
- Someone still matches once a week. Automatic removes the button. It should not remove the log.

A new loan, a new processor, or a new habit of running deposits through the rent link turns it back off. Review the choice quarterly, the same extend, hold, or retire decision as the rest of this series. A manual routine you have outgrown should be retired on purpose.

## The practical next step

1. Write down where automatic payout, if you use it, sends money, in words: which kind of account, not a number. Compare that sentence to the loan file with counsel if you are unsure.
2. List what else besides identified rent can land in that processor balance. If deposits can, stop the automatic sweep until they have a split.
3. Put the five-line release card on a weekday. Name the person who may release and the person who covers leave.
4. Define releasable cash in one sentence, including the return-window cushion, and leave that amount behind on the first two releases to see if it was enough.
5. Schedule a quarterly look: keep manual, or turn automatic back on, and record the reason in the log.
