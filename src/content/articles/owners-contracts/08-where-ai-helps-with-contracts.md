# Where AI Helps with Contracts, and Where a Lawyer Still Has to Read It

AI tools can read a stack of agreements in minutes, pull out the dates, and draft a tidy summary. That speed is useful. It is also where owners get into trouble, because a summary that reads well can feel like an answer. This closing article of the series gives you a contract task table: three lanes that sort each job into what AI may prepare, what a person on your team decides, and what counsel reads.

## Reading is a different job from interpreting

The series has treated contracts as operating documents. "Every Contract Is an Operating Manual: Reading for Obligations, Not Just Terms" read them for the work they create. The articles that followed turned that reading into a register, a certificate worksheet, a scope sheet, and a chain index. Each of those tools depends on reading carefully. None of them depends on deciding what the words mean in law.

AI fits the first job well. Reading a long agreement, finding every date, listing the parties as printed, locating the insurance section, comparing an amendment against the base document: these are preparation tasks. They involve extracting, organizing, comparing, and flagging. A person reviews the output against the source.

Interpreting is a different job. What a clause requires, whether a notice was effective, which of two conflicting documents controls, whether a term can be enforced, how to answer the other side's position: those are legal questions. An AI tool can produce fluent text about any of them. Fluent text is not a reading by someone accountable for the answer. Where legal meaning matters, counsel reads it.

## The contract task table

The table sorts common contract jobs into three lanes. It is a starting point for your own version.

| Contract job | AI may prepare | A person on your team decides | Counsel reads |
|---|---|---|---|
| Find every date and notice window | Extract dates and quote the clause | Confirm each date and add it to the register | When the timing language is unclear |
| Summarize an agreement for staff | Draft a plain-language summary with section references | Approve the summary for internal use | When the summary would support a position with the other side |
| Compare a certificate to the contract | Extract fields and flag mismatches | Request renewals and route questions | Contract language, alongside the insurance advisor |
| Check vendor work against scope | Compare notes to the scope sheet and flag gaps | Accept or return the work | Acceptance or dispute language when stakes are high |
| Index an amended agreement | List documents, dates, parties as printed, sections touched | Verify every row against signed copies | Which document controls, and whether informal changes count |
| Draft a routine notice | Prepare a first draft that quotes the governing clause | Review, edit, and decide whether to send | Any notice with legal effect: termination, default, non-renewal, a claim |
| Respond to the other side's position | Organize the documents and the timeline | Decide to bring in counsel | The response itself |

Read the right-hand column first. Anything that lands there goes to counsel before it goes anywhere else. The middle column is always a named person. The left column never sends, signs, approves, or decides.

### Where AI-prepared contract work goes wrong

Even inside the left column, the preparation can miss. Four patterns are worth watching for, because each one produces output that looks finished.

- **Paraphrase drift.** A summary restates a clause in smoother words and quietly changes a condition, a party, or a deadline. Ask for quotations beside every summary line.
- **Missing attachments.** The tool reads the base agreement and never sees the exhibit, the amendment, or the side letter. Its answer is accurate about the document it read and incomplete about the agreement.
- **Stale versions.** The folder holds an unsigned draft beside the executed copy, and the tool reads the draft. The chain index is the check: every AI answer should name which document in the family it came from.
- **Confident answers to legal questions.** Asked what a clause means, a tool may answer in a clear, assured voice. The assurance is a feature of the writing. Treat the answer as a question for counsel, already typed out.

## Setting the boundary in practice

A table on paper is only useful if the workflow follows it. A few habits make the lanes real.

- **Every AI output cites its source.** A summary that does not point to the section it came from cannot be checked, so it cannot be used.
- **Drafts are marked as drafts.** Anything AI prepares carries a label until a person has reviewed it.
- **Nothing leaves the building unreviewed.** A notice, a reply, or a request to another party goes through a named person, every time.
- **Legal questions are written down, not answered.** When an AI tool or a team member reaches a question in the right-hand column, it becomes a reader note for counsel, in the register or the chain index.
- **Counsel gets organized packages.** The payoff of the earlier tools is that counsel reads a clean set: the index, the relevant excerpts, the dates, and the specific question.

Take the illustrative regional landscaping company with commercial and municipal service contracts. Before the spring season, an AI tool reads its customer contracts and prepares a list of renewal dates, insurance sections, and scope descriptions, each with a section reference. The office manager checks the list against the signed copies and updates the register. Two contracts have notice language the manager cannot read with confidence, and one municipal contract references a document nobody can locate. Those three items go to counsel together, with the index attached. The AI did hours of reading. The judgment stayed with people.

An illustrative property management office for a few apartment communities could draw the same line. AI drafts the routine vendor reminder, a manager reviews and sends it, and anything touching a lease default goes to counsel before a word is drafted.

### The view from Arnould Blvd

On The Blvd Shopping Center runs on Command Platform, and its AI concierge is drawn to exactly this boundary. It answers against the property's own documents, not the open internet. It can summarize a clause, find the governing document, and draft a first-pass notice for review. It does not negotiate, approve, or send. When a question about a lease or a recorded instrument needs a legal reading, the concierge's job is to put the right document in front of the right person. The operator decides what to do next, and counsel reads what needs a lawyer.

## What the series adds up to

Across eight articles, this series has built a small set of tools that work together. The operating-manual reading from the first article finds the obligations. "The Clauses That Create Recurring Work: Notice, Renewal, Insurance, and Reporting" and "Dates That Run Themselves: Auto-Renewals, Notice Windows, and Evergreen Terms" show where the recurring work and the dates sit. "The Obligation Register: One Table for Everything Your Contracts Require" gives them one home. The certificate worksheet, the scope sheet and change log, and the chain index keep specific kinds of paper current and usable. This article draws the line around all of them.

The pattern holds at every step. Read the paper in its own words. Record what it asks of you, with a source. Name the person who owns each item. Send legal questions to counsel with the documents organized. AI can make each of those steps faster. It does not change who is accountable for them.

## The practical next step

1. Copy the contract task table and adjust the rows to the contract jobs your business actually does.
2. For each row, name the person in the middle column, and confirm who your counsel is for the right-hand column.
3. Adopt the five habits: cited sources, labeled drafts, no unreviewed outbound messages, written legal questions, and organized packages for counsel.
4. Gather the open reader notes from your register, certificate worksheet, scope sheets, and chain indexes, and send them to counsel as one package.
5. Schedule a quarterly review of the whole contract system, register, worksheets, and task table, with the people who use it.
