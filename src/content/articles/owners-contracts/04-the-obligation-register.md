# The Obligation Register: One Table for Everything Your Contracts Require

Part I of this series read contracts one at a time: for obligations, for recurring clauses, for dates that act on their own. The result is a stack of worksheets, one per document. This article combines them into a single obligation register, explains the difference between dated and standing obligations, and shows how to run the register as a weekly operating tool rather than a file that gets built once and forgotten.

## From a stack of worksheets to one table

A worksheet answers "what does this contract require?" An owner also needs the answer to a different question: "what do all of our contracts require of us this month, and who is doing it?" The first question is about a document. The second is about the business. Only one table can answer it.

The illustrative landscaping company from earlier articles, a regional landscaping company with commercial and municipal service contracts, shows why. After Part I it holds worksheets for a city parks agreement, an office park agreement, a snow-and-ice addendum with a medical campus, a fleet lease, a telematics subscription, and several subcontracts. Each worksheet is accurate. None of them shows that the city's monthly service log, the office park's quarterly walk-through report, and the insurance certificate for the medical campus all fall due in the same week in October, and that the office manager owns all three.

The register puts those rows side by side. It does not replace the worksheets or the documents. It points to them. Each row is one obligation, and each row cites the executed document and section it came from, so anyone can check the register against the paper.

The same structure fits any owner-led business with a contract file. An illustrative dental group with four offices would register its lab agreements, equipment leases, software licenses, and office leases in one table. A small distributor would register supplier terms, customer terms, and its warehouse lease. The rows differ. The columns do not.

## The register's columns

Keep the register narrow enough to read on one screen. Ten columns cover most businesses.

| Column | What it holds |
|---|---|
| ID | A short code so rows can be referenced in meetings and notes |
| Source | Document name, section, and date of the executed version |
| Obligation | Verb and object in plain words: "Submit monthly service log" |
| Who acts | The party the document names |
| Internal owner | The person on your team who does it, plus a backup |
| Type | Dated, recurring, or standing |
| Next due or checkpoint | The next date, or the decision point where a standing obligation is checked |
| Evidence | What shows it was done, and where that evidence is kept |
| Status | Open, done, waiting on the other party, or question pending |
| Counsel flag | A short note when meaning is unclear and the question has gone to counsel |

The "who acts" and "internal owner" columns stay separate, as they were on the worksheet. The "counsel flag" column is where the line between context and counsel is kept in practice. The register tracks the question and its status. It never records an answer that did not come from counsel.

## Dated obligations and standing obligations

Most rows in a register have dates. A report is due on the 10th. A certificate lapses in June. A notice window opens in January. These are **dated** obligations, and a recurring obligation is simply a dated one that repeats.

Some rows have no date at all. They describe a condition that applies every time a certain kind of decision is made. Call these **standing** obligations. A municipal contract might say subcontracting requires the city's prior written approval. There is no due date. There is a checkpoint: every time the landscaping company considers adding a subcontractor to that account, the row applies. A supply agreement might restrict where a product may be sold. A franchise-style agreement might require using approved vendors. Each one is silent most days and decisive on the day it applies.

Standing obligations are the easiest to lose in a calendar, because a calendar only holds dates. The register holds them by listing the decision that triggers the check in the "next due or checkpoint" column: "any new subcontractor on the city account," "any change of use," "any new vendor for the medical campus." Then the decision process itself, the proposal template or the approval step, points back to the register row.

## An illustrative register

Here is a slice of the landscaping company's register. Every row is illustrative and not taken from any real contract.

| ID | Source | Obligation | Type | Internal owner | Next due or checkpoint | Evidence | Status | Counsel flag |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| CP-01 | City parks, §6.2 | Submit monthly service log | Recurring | Office manager | October 10 | Emailed log, saved to account folder | Open | None |
| CP-02 | City parks, §9.1 | Keep insurance certificate current | Dated | Owner | Policy renewal, June | Certificate on file with the city | Done | Coverage question sent |
| CP-03 | City parks, §11 | Obtain approval before subcontracting | Standing | Owner | Any new subcontractor on this account | Written approval in folder | Open | None |
| OP-01 | Office park, §3 | Quarterly walk-through report | Recurring | Account manager | October 15 | Signed report | Open | None |
| OP-02 | Office park, §12 | Decide on renewal | Dated | Owner | Decision date, December 15 | Decision note | Open | Window counting pending |
| MC-01 | Medical campus addendum, §2 | Submit snow season price proposal | Recurring | Owner | September 30 | Proposal and delivery record | Done | None |
| FL-01 | Fleet lease, §7 | Report vehicle mileage | Recurring | Operations manager | Quarterly | Mileage statement | Waiting on telematics export | None |

Read the owner column and the October load is plain: the office manager and account manager both have reports due in the same week. Read the counsel column and two open questions are visible without anyone having to remember them. Read the type column and one standing row, CP-03, is waiting for the next time a subcontractor is proposed.

## Running the register

A register built once and never reviewed drifts from the paper within a season. Three habits keep it current.

- **A weekly review.** Fifteen minutes with the internal owners: what is due in the next two weeks, what is waiting on the other party, what closed. Update status in the meeting.
- **A document trigger.** When a contract is signed, amended, renewed, or ended, its rows are added, revised, or closed the same week, citing the new version. "Amendments, Side Letters, and the Version You Can Actually Rely On," later in this series, covers how to keep that chain intact.
- **A monthly owner read.** The owner scans standing obligations and counsel flags, not only the dated rows. Those are the rows that do not announce themselves.

AI tools can prepare much of the first draft. They can extract candidate obligations from a stack of agreements, organize them into register rows, and flag rows whose source section has changed in a new version. A person verifies every row against the executed document before it goes live, and nothing the tool drafts is sent to a counterparty without review. The article "Where AI Helps with Contracts, and Where a Lawyer Still Has to Read It" takes up that boundary in detail. Two recurring categories get their own treatment first: "Certificates of Insurance: The Recurring Paper Most Owners Stop Checking" and "Vendor and Service Agreements: Scope, Change Orders, and What 'Complete' Means."

## The practical next step

1. Set up a ten-column register in a shared spreadsheet and move every row from your Part I worksheets into it, citing the executed source.
2. Mark each row dated, recurring, or standing, and write a checkpoint for every standing row.
3. Name an internal owner and a backup for every row, then sort by owner and next due date to see where the load falls.
4. Hold the first fifteen-minute weekly review this month and update status in the meeting.
5. Add a step to your contract-signing routine: no document is filed until its rows are in the register.
