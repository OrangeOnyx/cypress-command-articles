# Dates That Run Themselves: Auto-Renewals, Notice Windows, and Evergreen Terms

Most contract dates wait for someone to act. A few do the opposite: they produce an outcome on their own unless someone acts first, inside a window that may close months before the date itself. This article describes three kinds of self-running dates and gives you a four-date worksheet that turns each one into a decision the owner makes on purpose.

## Three kinds of self-running dates

The previous article, "The Clauses That Create Recurring Work: Notice, Renewal, Insurance, and Reporting," listed renewal as one of four clause families that keep producing work. This article looks closely at the renewal clauses that move without anyone's hand on them. They come in three common shapes. The labels below are plain operating descriptions, not legal categories, and how any particular clause works is a question for counsel.

- **Auto-renewal.** The document says the term renews for another period unless a party gives notice. If nobody acts, the contract continues on the stated terms, which may include a stated price change.
- **Notice window.** The document ties an action to a period before a date: "not less than 60 days prior," "no earlier than 120 and no later than 90 days before." The date that matters to the owner is the edge of the window, not the end of the term.
- **Evergreen term.** The document runs period to period with no fixed end, until a party acts in the way the text describes. There may be no date on the calendar at all unless someone puts one there.

All three are easy to live with when they are visible. The risk is invisibility. A software subscription renews for a year because the notice window closed during a busy month. A vendor agreement continues at a price nobody reviewed. A customer contract the owner meant to reprice rolls forward on last year's rate.

Return to the illustrative landscaping company with commercial and municipal service contracts. On the customer side, its office park agreement renews each year unless either party gives notice within a stated window. On the vendor side, it holds a fleet lease, a telematics subscription for its trucks, a uniform service, and an equipment maintenance plan, and at least two of them renew on their own. The owner sits on both sides of the self-running date.

## Count backward from the event

The fix is a worksheet that starts at the event and counts backward to the day someone has to begin working on it. Every self-running date gets four dates.

| Date | What it is | How to set it |
|---|---|---|
| Event date | The date the document says the term ends or renews | Read from the executed document and its amendments |
| Notice deadline | The last day to act under the notice window | Count back from the event as the document states; confirm the counting method with counsel |
| Decision date | The day the owner decides: renew, reprice, or end | Set it early enough to prepare the notice and get it reviewed |
| Preparation start | The day someone begins gathering what the decision needs | Set it early enough to collect usage, cost, performance, and alternatives |

Two columns belong beside those dates: the internal owner and the source section. The owner is the person who will make or bring the decision. The source section lets anyone check the worksheet against the paper.

Counting is where readings differ. Calendar days or business days, the day notice is sent or the day it is received, what happens when a deadline lands on a weekend: these are questions about meaning, and the answers belong to counsel. Until you have an answer, write down each candidate date the language could produce and calendar the earliest one. That keeps the question open without letting the window close while you wait.

## An illustrative worked example

Here is how the landscaping company's worksheet might look for three agreements. The dates, windows, and terms are illustrative and not taken from any real contract.

| Agreement | Shape | Event date | Notice deadline | Decision date | Preparation start | Owner |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Office park grounds agreement | Auto-renewal, one-year periods, 60-day window | March 31 | Early January to end of January, depending on counting | December 15 | November 1 | Owner |
| Telematics subscription | Auto-renewal, annual, 30-day window | August 1 | Early July | June 15 | May 15 | Operations manager |
| Uniform service | Evergreen, month to month, 30 days' notice | None fixed | Rolling | Reviewed each January | December 1 | Office manager |

Read across the first row. The office park agreement ends in spring, but the real work starts in the fall. The preparation start is when the account manager pulls a season of service records, crew hours, and complaints. The decision date gives the owner time to decide whether to renew, propose a new price, or let the agreement end, and to have any notice reviewed before it goes out. The notice deadline shows a range because the counting question is still with counsel.

The evergreen row has no event date, so the worksheet assigns a review date instead. A contract that never ends on its own still deserves a regular look.

The same worksheet serves an illustrative dental group reviewing its imaging software license, or a restaurant owner reviewing a linen contract. The shapes are the same. Only the names change.

## What the calendar has to hold

A worksheet is only useful if its dates reach the people who act on them. A few habits make that dependable.

- **One calendar, not many.** Put every preparation start, decision date, and notice deadline on a shared calendar the owner reviews, not in individual inboxes.
- **A named owner and a backup.** A self-running date with one person attached will run itself during that person's vacation.
- **Reminders at the preparation start, not the deadline.** A reminder on the notice deadline arrives too late to prepare anything.
- **A record of the decision.** When the owner decides to renew, write it down with the date. "We chose to renew" and "nobody noticed" look the same on the invoice and very different in a review.
- **Recount after every amendment.** An amendment can change the term, the window, or the notice address. When the paper changes, the four dates change.

AI tools can help with the first pass: extract candidate renewal and notice language from a stack of agreements and prepare a draft worksheet. A person then checks each row against the executed document. The article "Where AI Helps with Contracts, and Where a Lawyer Still Has to Read It," later in this series, covers that boundary.

## Dates measured in years

Not every self-running date arrives within a year. Some documents run for decades, and the date that matters may be a decade out when the worksheet is first built. Those long dates are the easiest to lose, because no one currently on the team may be there when they arrive.

### The view from Arnould Blvd

At On The Blvd, the reciprocal access and parking servitude with the corner bank parcel is recorded as Entry 2004-00057697. Under it, the center counts 13 spaces on the bank parcel, and the servitude expires on December 30, 2034. That date is years away, and it sits on the critical-dates board in Command Platform today, alongside obligations due next month. Parking decisions made now can depend on spaces whose right to be counted has an end date, so the date has to be visible long before it arrives. The four-date worksheet applies unchanged: an event date in 2034, and a preparation start set far enough ahead to review what the center relies on and, with counsel, what options the document leaves open.

## The practical next step

1. List every contract you hold, as customer or as vendor, that renews or continues unless someone acts.
2. For each one, fill in the four dates: event, notice deadline, decision, and preparation start, citing the source section.
3. Where the counting method is unclear, write down each candidate date, calendar the earliest, and send the question to counsel.
4. Put every preparation start and decision date on one shared calendar with a named owner and a backup.
5. Add any date more than three years out to the same calendar now, with a preparation start of its own.
