# Every Contract Is an Operating Manual: Reading for Obligations, Not Just Terms

Most owners read a contract closely twice: once before signing, for price and term, and again when something goes wrong. In between, the document sits in a folder while the business performs it from memory. This article opens The Owner's Contracts Series with a second way to read the same paper, as a list of things someone must do, by when, with what proof, and a one-page worksheet to capture what you find.

## Terms and obligations are different reading jobs

A term is a fact the parties agreed on: the price, the start date, the length, the service area. An obligation is an action someone must take: send a report, renew a certificate, give notice, inspect a site, invoice in a stated format. Terms answer "what did we agree to?" Obligations answer "what does someone have to do this week?"

Most contract summaries capture terms. The deal sheet lists the price, the term, and the renewal option. It rarely lists the monthly report the customer expects by a set day, the photo record of each completed site, or the named contact who must receive notices. Those lines are where recurring work lives, and they are the lines a team tends to perform from memory.

Throughout this series we use one illustrative running example: a regional landscaping company with commercial and municipal service contracts. It is hypothetical, not a client. Picture its contract file: a mowing and bed-maintenance agreement with a city parks department, a grounds agreement with an office park, a snow-and-ice addendum with a medical campus, and a handful of subcontracts for irrigation and tree work. Read for terms, the file describes prices and seasons. Read for obligations, it describes a year of work: crew-level tasks, office-level reports, and owner-level dates.

Neither reading replaces a lawyer's. What a clause means legally, whether it can be enforced, and how it interacts with other documents are questions for counsel. The obligation reading is an operator's job: find the actions, name who does them, and note where the language is unclear enough that counsel should read it.

## Read with a pencil, looking for verbs

An obligation reading is slower than a skim and faster than it sounds. Work section by section and mark four kinds of language.

- **Action verbs attached to a party.** "Contractor shall submit," "Owner will provide," "Vendor must maintain." Circle the party and underline the verb.
- **Time words.** "Within," "no later than," "prior to," "annually," "upon." These turn an action into a date or a trigger.
- **Evidence words.** "Written," "certified," "in the form attached," "with a copy to." These describe what performance has to look like on paper.
- **Cross-references.** "As set forth in Exhibit C," "subject to Section 12." Each one points to another page the obligation depends on.

Do not interpret while you mark. The first pass is an inventory. If a sentence could be read two ways, mark it with a question and move on. That question goes to counsel later, in writing, with the section number attached.

Two habits keep the reading complete. Read the exhibits, schedules, and attachments with the same care as the body, because site lists and reporting formats often live there. And read the whole document, including the general provisions near the end, because notice addresses and reporting contacts usually sit in those sections.

## The obligation worksheet

Each marked obligation becomes one row. Seven columns are enough.

| Column | What to write | Example entry |
|---|---|---|
| Source | Document, section, and page | "City parks agreement, §6.2, p. 9" |
| Who acts | The party the text names | "Contractor" |
| Action | Verb and object, in plain words | "Submit monthly service log" |
| Timing | Date, frequency, or trigger | "By the 10th of each month" |
| Evidence | What shows it was done | "Log in the attached format, emailed to the named contact" |
| Internal owner | The person on your team who does it | "Office manager" |
| Question for counsel | Anything unclear about meaning | "Does the log include the irrigation subcontractor's visits?" |

The "who acts" column records what the document says. The "internal owner" column records who in your business will actually do it. Keep those separate. A contract may say "Contractor" on every page, but a contractor is a company, and a company does nothing until a person is named.

The last column is where the line between context and counsel shows up in practice. The worksheet does not answer legal questions. It collects them, tied to their sections, so a lawyer's time goes to the language that needs it.

## An illustrative worked example

Here is how the city parks agreement might look after an afternoon with the worksheet. The clauses are illustrative, paraphrased the way a reader would summarize them, and are not taken from any real contract.

| Source | Who acts | Action | Timing | Evidence | Internal owner | Question for counsel |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| §4.1 | Contractor | Mow listed parks | Weekly, April through October | Crew completion sheet | Crew lead, north route | None |
| §4.3 | Contractor | Report damaged irrigation heads | Within 48 hours of discovery | Written notice with photo | Crew lead, north route | Discovery by whom: crew or subcontractor? |
| §6.2 | Contractor | Submit monthly service log | By the 10th | Exhibit B format | Office manager | None |
| §9.1 | Contractor | Keep insurance certificate current | Before expiry | Certificate on file with the city | Owner | Which coverages must the certificate show? |
| §14 | Either party | Give notice of non-renewal | Stated days before term end | Written, to the address in §17 | Owner | How is the window counted? |
| Ex. A | City | Provide updated park list | Each season | Revised exhibit | Owner receives | Does a new park change the price? |

Read the table down its columns and three patterns appear. The timing column spans crew-level work (weekly), office-level work (monthly), and owner-level work (once a term), which is why a single "contracts person" rarely keeps up with all of it. The internal owner column shows the owner carrying the rare, high-stakes rows, which works only if the owner has a calendar that surfaces them. And the counsel column holds three focused questions instead of a general request to "look over the contract."

Note the last row. Obligations run both ways. When the other party owes you something, a list, an approval, a payment, it belongs on the worksheet too, because your own work often waits on it. A dental office reading its lab services agreement, or a distributor reading a supplier contract, will find the same thing: half the schedule depends on what the other side sends and when.

## Why the paper has to be the source

A worksheet is only as good as the document it came from. Summaries drift. A deal memo written at signing may not reflect the amendment signed a year and a half later. A spreadsheet built from memory may carry the price from the prior contract. The rule is simple: every row cites the executed document by section, and when the document changes, the row changes.

This takes discipline in two places. First, confirm you are reading the signed version, with every amendment, and not the last draft someone emailed. Second, when a worksheet entry and the paper disagree, the paper wins and the worksheet gets fixed. The article "Amendments, Side Letters, and the Version You Can Actually Rely On" later in this series covers how to keep that chain intact.

## The practical next step

1. Pick one contract your team performs every week and set aside two hours to read it for obligations rather than terms.
2. Mark every action verb attached to a party, every time word, and every evidence word, including in the exhibits.
3. Fill the seven-column worksheet, one row per obligation, citing the section of the executed document.
4. Name an internal owner for every row. Where no one is obvious, that row is your first finding.
5. Collect the questions in the last column and send them to counsel as one short list, each tied to its section.
