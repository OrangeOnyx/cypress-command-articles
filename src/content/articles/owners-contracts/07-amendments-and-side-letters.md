# Amendments, Side Letters, and the Version You Can Actually Rely On

Few agreements stay as signed. Over the years they collect amendments, extensions, side letters, consents, and email confirmations, and the working copy on someone's desk drifts from the set of documents that were actually executed. This article gives you a contract chain index: one page per agreement that lists every document in the family, in order, with what each one touches. It does not tell you which document controls. It makes sure the person who answers that question is reading the whole set.

## An agreement is a family of documents

The signed base agreement is where most people stop reading. The paper that governs day-to-day work may be spread across several later documents.

- **Amendments** change specific terms and are usually signed by both parties.
- **Extensions and renewals** may be separate documents, amendments, or letters.
- **Side letters** address a point outside the main document, often a concession or a clarification agreed at signing.
- **Consents and waivers** approve something the agreement otherwise restricts, sometimes for a single occasion.
- **Assignments** move the agreement, or part of it, to a different party.
- **Correspondence** that one side treats as an agreement and the other may not.

Whether each of these changes the agreement, and in what order they take effect, are legal questions. What each one means for you is for counsel. The operating question comes first: do you have all of them, and does everyone know which ones exist?

Take the illustrative regional landscaping company with commercial and municipal service contracts. Its largest commercial contract might have been signed years ago, extended twice by letter, amended once to add a site, and adjusted by an email that reduced the mowing frequency during a drought. The crew works from the drought email. The billing office works from the base agreement's price schedule. Neither is wrong about what it has. Each is working from part of the family.

## The contract chain index

The index lists the whole family on one page. The rows below are illustrative.

| # | Document | Date signed | Parties as named | Sections touched | Summary (plain words) | Signed copy location | Reader notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Base service agreement | Year one | As printed | All | Original terms | Contracts drive, folder A | None |
| 2 | First extension letter | Year three | As printed | Term | Extends term | Contracts drive, folder A | None |
| 3 | Amendment No. 1 | Year four | As printed | Scope, pricing | Adds second site | Scanned signed copy | None |
| 4 | Email on mowing frequency | Year five | Office manager to customer | Scope | Reduces frequency during drought | Inbox, saved as PDF | Effect unclear; counsel to read |

Five rules keep it honest.

- **List in signing order.** Date order shows the sequence in which the family grew, which is what a reader needs first.
- **Name parties exactly as printed.** If an entity name changed between documents, the index shows each name as its document prints it.
- **Keep the summary modest.** The summary helps someone find the right document. It is never the document.
- **Record where the signed copy lives.** An unsigned draft in a shared folder is a common source of confusion. The index points to the executed copy, or says it cannot be found.
- **Use the reader-notes column for questions.** Any document whose effect is unclear gets a note and a named reader.

Each dated item the family creates, such as a new expiration or notice deadline, also moves into the register from "The Obligation Register: One Table for Everything Your Contracts Require."

### Keeping the index current

An index built once and never touched becomes one more stale summary. Three habits keep it alive.

- **Row before file.** Any new document in the family gets its row on the day it is signed, before the signed copy is filed away.
- **One owner per index.** A named person keeps each index. Others can suggest changes; the owner makes them.
- **Check at renewal.** Before any renewal, extension, or renegotiation, the owner reads the index top to bottom and confirms each signed copy is still where the index says it is.

An illustrative regional staffing agency with a master services agreement for each large customer might keep one index per customer and review all of them each January. The review takes an afternoon. It also tends to surface the side letter someone signed in the spring and never mentioned.

## Keep the document's own words

The index is only as good as its fidelity to the paper. The most tempting error is tidying: correcting a name that looks misspelled, normalizing an address, updating a party to its current name, or merging two documents' descriptions into one smooth summary. Each tidy edit makes the record easier to read and weaker as a record.

Keep a clear line between what the document says and what you have concluded. Quote the document's words when they matter. Put your own notes in a separate column. If two documents describe the same thing differently, record both descriptions and flag the difference for a reader. Do not pick one.

An illustrative family-owned manufacturer with a long-running equipment lease shows why. The lease names the lessee under a company name that was later changed. The amendment uses the new name. A clean summary would use the current name throughout. The index keeps both, as printed, and notes the change, so whoever reads the chain later can see exactly what each document said.

### The view from Arnould Blvd

The center's street is Arnould Blvd. The recorded subdivision the center sits in is "Arnold Heights Subd. Ext. No. 1." These are two distinct names. The subdivision's name is a legal name of its own, not a misspelling of the street. A well-meaning edit that "corrected" one to match the other would break the chain between the property's records and the recorded documents they rely on. The operating rule at On The Blvd is that every record preserves each document's own words. The street stays Arnould wherever the street is meant. The subdivision stays "Arnold Heights Subd. Ext. No. 1" wherever a recorded document uses it. Consistency comes from quoting accurately, and two sources are never edited to agree.

## When the chain has gaps

Most indexes turn up gaps on the first pass: an amendment referenced in a later document that nobody can find, a side letter mentioned in an email, a missing signature page. The index records each gap as an open item with an owner and a date to resolve it.

- **Missing executed copies.** Ask the other party, the broker, or the attorney who handled the signing for a copy.
- **References to documents you do not have.** List them, with the sentence that references them, quoted exactly.
- **Informal changes.** Emails and verbal arrangements the team relies on go in the index with a reader note. Whether they changed anything is for counsel to say.
- **Conflicting terms.** Two documents that say different things about the same point get one row each and a shared note.

An illustrative veterinary practice with two clinics and a shared equipment maintenance agreement might find, on its first pass, that the second clinic was added by a letter nobody countersigned. The index does not settle what that letter did. It puts the letter, the base agreement, and the question in front of counsel together.

AI can help build the first draft of an index. It can extract dates, parties, and section references from a folder of documents, compare them, and flag differences for review. It should quote rather than paraphrase, and a person checks every row against the signed copy before the index is relied on.

## The practical next step

1. Pick one agreement your business depends on and gather every document in its family, including emails the team treats as changes.
2. Build the chain index in signing order, with parties named exactly as printed and the location of each signed copy.
3. Mark every gap, conflict, and informal change with a reader note and a named person.
4. Send the reader notes to counsel as one package, with the index attached, instead of one question at a time.
5. Adopt a rule that no record is "corrected" to match another source without a note showing both versions.
