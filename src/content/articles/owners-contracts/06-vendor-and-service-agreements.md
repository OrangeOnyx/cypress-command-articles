# Vendor and Service Agreements: Scope, Change Orders, and What "Complete" Means

A service agreement describes work in a few paragraphs. The work itself happens across a season of visits, calls, and small requests that never make it back to the paper. This article gives you two tools that keep them connected: a scope sheet that turns the agreement's description into a list you can check against, and a change log that records every request outside that list. Together they make "complete" something you can point to.

## Where scope lives in the paper

Scope is rarely in one place. In a typical service agreement it is spread across a description of services, an exhibit or schedule, a pricing page, and sometimes a set of specifications or a proposal attached by reference. Each piece says something about what the vendor will do, how often, and to what standard.

Reading for scope means pulling those pieces together. For each item of work, find:

- **The task**, in the agreement's own words.
- **The frequency or trigger**: weekly, monthly, seasonal, on request, or after a named event.
- **The standard**: any stated measure of what finished work looks like.
- **The location or asset**: which building, area, or piece of equipment.
- **The source**: the section, exhibit, or attachment where you found it.

Take the illustrative regional landscaping company with commercial and municipal service contracts, this time reading its own paper as the vendor. One municipal contract might describe mowing by frequency, a commercial contract might describe it by grass height, and a proposal attached to a third might describe it by visits per month. Three contracts, one task, three descriptions. The crew leader needs to know which one applies at which site, and the office needs to know what each customer expects to see.

When the pieces disagree with each other, or an attachment is referenced and cannot be found, that is a finding. Which piece controls is a question for counsel. The scope sheet records the disagreement so the question gets asked.

## The scope sheet

The scope sheet turns the agreement into rows: one row per task, per location. The entries below are illustrative.

| Task (agreement's words) | Location | Frequency / trigger | Stated standard | Source | Owner-side contact |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| "Mow and trim all turf areas" | Front lawn, both entrances | Weekly, growing season | "Uniform appearance" | Exhibit A, item 1 | Property manager |
| "Inspect irrigation system" | All zones | Monthly | Not stated | Exhibit A, item 4 | Property manager |
| "Seasonal color installation" | Entrance beds | Twice yearly | Not stated | Proposal, page 2 | Owner |

Two columns do most of the work. **Stated standard** shows where the agreement says what finished looks like, and where it says nothing. A blank there is common. It is also where disagreement tends to start later, because each side fills the blank with its own expectation. **Source** keeps every row tied to the paper, so nobody works from a summary of a summary.

The sheet does not interpret the agreement. It is a reading aid that lets the owner, the vendor, and the person who walks the site look at the same list. When the agreement changes, the sheet is updated from the new paper, not from memory. Every task with a date or a cycle also gets a row in the register from "The Obligation Register: One Table for Everything Your Contracts Require."

### Walk the sheet with the vendor

The sheet does its best work before the season starts. A thirty-minute walk of the site with the vendor's supervisor, sheet in hand, turns each row into a shared picture. The vendor points out rows it reads differently. The owner points out areas the sheet does not mention at all. Neither side changes the agreement on the walk. Both sides leave with a written list of the places where the paper and the site disagree, and anything on that list that touches the agreement's terms goes to the people who handle the contract. The landscaping company can run the same walk from the vendor side with its own customers, and many customers appreciate being asked.

## Change orders and the change log

Work outside the scope arrives in small pieces. A tenant asks for an extra cleanup before an event. A storm leaves debris. The owner asks the crew to take a look at a drainage problem while they are on site. Each request is reasonable. Each one is also work the scope sheet does not describe.

Many agreements describe how changes are requested and approved. Some call for a written change order; some are silent. What the agreement's change language means, and what happens when it is not followed, are questions for counsel. The operating job is simpler: record every out-of-scope request in one place, the same day it is made. The rows below are illustrative.

| Date | Requested by | Request | In scope? | Written approval | Price or basis | Status |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Last Tuesday | Property manager | Storm debris removal, rear lot | No, not on scope sheet | Email, same day | Quoted, accepted | Done, photo on file |
| Last Friday | Owner | Look at drainage near rear entrance | Unclear | None yet | Not discussed | Open, owner to decide |

The "In scope?" column is answered against the scope sheet, not against recollection. "Unclear" is a legitimate answer and a useful one, because it tells the owner a decision is waiting. An illustrative restaurant group with four locations and a shared equipment service vendor could run the same log. Every emergency call outside the maintenance agreement gets a row, and the monthly invoice is read against the log instead of against memory.

## What "complete" means

"Complete" has three parts, and each needs a home.

1. **The standard.** What the scope sheet says finished looks like, or a note that the agreement is silent.
2. **The evidence.** A photo, a checklist, a signed ticket, a meter reading: whatever shows the work happened.
3. **The acceptance.** A named person on the owner's side who confirms the work and the date.

Many agreements say little about acceptance. Where an agreement does address inspection, acceptance, or punch lists, that language deserves a careful reading, and counsel should read it when the stakes are high. The operating record is separate from its legal effect. It simply shows who looked, when, and what they saw.

A completion record for any single work order should answer five questions:

- Which scope sheet row or change log entry does this work belong to?
- What evidence is attached, and who provided it?
- Who on the owner's side looked at the work, and on what date?
- Was anything returned for correction, and when was it finished?
- Does the invoice line match the row it bills against?

AI can help here. It can compare a vendor's completion notes against the scope sheet, flag rows with no evidence attached, and draft a short summary for the person who accepts the work. It does not accept work, and it does not tell a vendor the job is done.

## The practical next step

1. Choose your largest recurring service agreement and build a scope sheet from it, one row per task per location, with a source for every row.
2. Mark every row where the stated standard is blank, and name who on your side will describe what finished looks like in practice.
3. Start a change log today and record every out-of-scope request from this month.
4. Name the person who accepts completed work for each vendor, and ask each vendor what evidence they can attach.
5. Send any conflicting or missing contract language you found to counsel before the next renewal, while nothing is in dispute.
