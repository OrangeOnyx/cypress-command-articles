# Certificates of Insurance: The Recurring Paper Most Owners Stop Checking

Most owners collect a certificate of insurance when a vendor starts or a tenant moves in. Then the file closes, the policy renews or does not, and the certificate in the drawer describes a year that has already ended. This article gives you a certificate worksheet: one row per party, the contract's own words beside what the certificate shows, and a monthly rhythm that keeps the paper current. It is a tracking tool. Whether the coverage is adequate is a question for your insurance advisor and, where contract language is involved, for counsel.

## Why certificates drift

A certificate of insurance is a one- or two-page summary, usually issued by the insured party's agent or broker, that lists the policies in force on the day it was issued. It is a snapshot. Policies renew on their own calendar, carriers change, limits change, and a vendor may switch agents between one season and the next. None of that reaches the owner unless someone asks.

The paper also arrives at the moments when everyone is busy with something else. A new vendor is being onboarded. A tenant is getting keys. The certificate is one item on a long list, and once it is in the file, the list feels finished.

Take the illustrative example this series has used from the start: a regional landscaping company with commercial and municipal service contracts. It sits on both sides of the certificate. Its own customers ask it for certificates before a season begins. It asks its subcontractors, a tree service and an irrigation contractor, for theirs. If the office tracks the certificates it sends and never tracks the ones it receives, half of the picture is missing.

The drift is ordinary. It does not mean anyone was careless. It means the certificate has an expiration date and the contract does not remind anyone about it. "The Clauses That Create Recurring Work: Notice, Renewal, Insurance, and Reporting" placed insurance among the clauses that generate work on a cycle. The certificate is where that cycle shows up on paper.

## What a certificate shows, and what it does not

Before tracking certificates, it helps to know what to read on one. Most certificate forms carry the same fields.

- **Insured.** The legal name of the party whose coverage is described. Compare it to the name on your contract, character for character.
- **Producer.** The agent or broker who issued the certificate. This is who you call when a new one is needed.
- **Insurers and policy numbers.** Each coverage line names a carrier and a policy number.
- **Coverage types and limits.** General liability, auto, workers' compensation, umbrella, and others, each with stated limits.
- **Effective and expiration dates.** The dates that make the certificate a recurring item.
- **Certificate holder.** The party the certificate was issued to. Check that it is you, spelled the way you appear in the contract.
- **Description of operations.** A free-text box that sometimes references a project, a location, or additional wording.

Many certificate forms also state on their face that they are issued for information only. What that statement means, what an endorsement does, and whether the coverage shown matches what your contract asks for are not questions a worksheet answers. Your insurance advisor reads coverage. Counsel reads contract language where its legal meaning matters. The worksheet makes sure both of them are looking at a current document.

## The certificate worksheet

The worksheet has one row per party and nine columns. Keep it beside the register from "The Obligation Register: One Table for Everything Your Contracts Require," and give each certificate expiration a row in that register, so the date lives in the same place as every other dated obligation.

| Column | What goes in it |
|---|---|
| Party | Vendor, tenant, or subcontractor, by legal name |
| Contract and section | The document and section that mention insurance |
| Contract's words | A short excerpt copied verbatim, not paraphrased |
| Certificate shows | Coverage types, limits, and holder as printed |
| Effective / expiration | Both dates, from the certificate |
| Match to read | "Looks consistent," "question," or "not yet compared" |
| Who reads it | Owner, insurance advisor, or counsel |
| Request sent | Date a renewal certificate was requested |
| Received | Date the current certificate arrived |

Three rules keep it useful.

- **Copy the contract's words.** A paraphrase such as "they need a million in liability" drifts from the source. The excerpt keeps the reader honest and lets an advisor see exactly what was written.
- **Mark "question," not conclusions.** When the certificate and the excerpt do not obviously line up, the row says "question" and names who reads it. The worksheet does not decide whether a gap matters.
- **Record the request date.** Most of the work is asking for the new certificate. A request date shows whether anyone asked.

Here is an illustrative slice of the landscaping company's received certificates.

| Party | Contract and section | Effective / expiration | Match to read | Who reads it | Request sent |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Tree service subcontractor | Subcontract, insurance section | Expires next month | Looks consistent | Owner | Not yet |
| Irrigation contractor | Master subcontract, exhibit | Expired last quarter | Not yet compared | Insurance advisor | Sent, no reply |
| Equipment rental yard | Rental agreement, insurance paragraph | Current | Question | Counsel | Not needed yet |

The expired row with no reply is the one to act on this week.

## A monthly rhythm

The worksheet only works if someone opens it. A short monthly routine is enough for most owner-led businesses.

1. **Sort by expiration.** Anything expiring in the next sixty days gets a renewal request, sent to the producer listed on the current certificate and copied to the party.
2. **Chase the silent rows.** Any request more than two weeks old without a reply gets a second request and a note in the row.
3. **Read what arrived.** New certificates are checked against the name, holder, dates, and the contract excerpt. Anything that does not obviously match becomes "question."
4. **Route the questions.** Coverage questions go to the insurance advisor. Contract-language questions go to counsel. The owner decides what happens with the answer.
5. **Add new parties.** Any vendor or tenant added this month gets a row before work starts or keys change hands.

An illustrative commercial cleaning company with a dozen office buildings under contract runs the same routine from the vendor side. It lists which customers asked for a certificate, sends renewals before the customers ask, and keeps the request dates. The rhythm is the same whether the business holds the paper or sends it.

AI can help with the reading. It can extract dates, names, and limits from a certificate, compare them to the worksheet, and flag a mismatch for a person to review. It does not decide that coverage is sufficient, and it does not send a request on its own.

## One place per party

A worksheet with many rows gets easier to run when each party has one place to look for its own items. The office stops re-sending the same request to three different email addresses, and the party stops guessing which document is current. The owner, in turn, has one row to check per party instead of a thread to reconstruct.

## The practical next step

1. List every vendor, tenant, and subcontractor whose contract mentions insurance, and start the worksheet with one row each.
2. For each row, copy the contract's insurance language verbatim and pull the most recent certificate on file.
3. Sort by expiration date and send renewal requests for anything expired or expiring within sixty days.
4. Mark every row where the certificate and the excerpt do not obviously match as "question," and route it to your insurance advisor or counsel.
5. Put a monthly thirty-minute review on the calendar and name the person who owns it.
