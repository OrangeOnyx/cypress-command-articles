# Tenant Default and the Louisiana File: Notice, Possession, and the Empty Bay

> Context, not counsel. This article is not legal advice and it is not an eviction procedure. It is the operating file for a commercial default: what to record, what not to do with the space, and what to hand a Louisiana lawyer. Notice periods, cure periods, and court steps below are the source draft's topics. Qualified Louisiana counsel confirms them against the current Code and the signed lease before any letter is sent or any petition is filed.

Default is the moment a lease stops being a rent roll line and becomes a set of dated tasks. The first days decide whether you have a clean record or a sequence you have to restart. They do not decide themselves. The lease says what notice looks like. Counsel says whether you are in a position to send it, and whether a court proceeding is the next step. The team says who owns each row.

Read this with "Louisiana Commercial Lease Law: What the Operator Tracks, and What Counsel Decides," which holds the citation list, and with "Security Deposits: Hold, Apply, and Refund Without Spending the Cash," which holds the deposit you must not spend while the default is open. Figures in the tables are illustrative ranges from the source draft. They are not a quote for your lawyer, your court, or your vacant suite.

## Two kinds of default, one file

Monetary default is a failure to pay a sum the lease makes due: base rent, recoveries, taxes, insurance, or another stated charge. Non-monetary default is a breach of some other promise: a use the lease does not allow, a maintenance failure, a sublease or assignment without the consent the lease requires, an exclusive the lease protects.

The file treats them differently because the leases usually do. Monetary defaults are easier to document, because the ledger and the clause can be laid side by side. Non-monetary defaults need a description of the fact, photographs or correspondence, and a cure that may depend on someone other than the tenant. Some leases recite defaults that counsel may treat as having a shorter cure, or none. That reading is counsel's. Your job is to bring the clause and the facts, not to skip the notice because the breach feels serious.

The management point still holds, and it is not a legal conclusion. A missed payment that produces no organized response teaches the tenant that the clock is informal. A missed payment that produces a same-week file, and a notice only after counsel has approved it, teaches the tenant that the lease is the clock. Send nothing that counsel has not seen. Do not skip the file because you are being patient. Patience is a workout you write down, not a silence.

## The notice is the clock

Before a remedy, the lease's notice clause has to be followed. Capture the method the lease names. Certified mail, hand delivery, and overnight courier are common. Email is enough only when the lease says it is enough. The source draft treats the cure period as running from receipt, not from the day you mailed the letter. Confirm that reading with counsel for the lease in front of you before you count days.

Illustrative cure bands from the source draft. They describe how commercial leases are often written. They are not Louisiana statutes, and they lose to the lease in your hand.

| Default the lease describes | Notice the file should hold | Illustrative cure in many forms | Note for counsel |
|---|---|---|---|
| Base rent | Written notice of the amount | Often 5–10 days | The lease may say something shorter or longer |
| Recoveries, taxes, insurance | Written notice of the amount | Often a longer window than base rent | Do not assume the rent cure covers these |
| Use | Written notice of the fact | Often about 30 days | The lease may add time if cure has started |
| Maintenance | Written notice of the fact | Often about 30 days | Some leases let the owner cure and charge back; counsel reads that sentence before anyone enters to repair |
| Sublease or assignment | Written notice | Whatever that lease says | Do not treat "immediate termination" as available until counsel says so |
| Apparent abandonment | A note of what you observed, and the date | Do not invent a right to re-enter | The source draft said no notice was required and that the owner could re-enter. This article does not adopt that instruction. Call counsel before anyone goes in |

## Possession is counsel's filing

The source draft describes Louisiana commercial eviction as a court process under Code of Civil Procedure articles 4701 through 4735, centered on a rule to vacate: a written notice to vacate, then a petition, a hearing, a judgment of possession if the landlord prevails, and a writ executed by the constable if the tenant remains. It sketches an uncontested path in roughly three weeks and a contested path in months. Those day counts are unverified here.

What the operator can prepare, so counsel is not reconstructing the story from email:

| What counsel will ask for | What the file should already contain |
|---|---|
| The debt or the breach | Ledger pages, the clause, and a short chronology |
| The notice | The letter counsel approved, the method, and proof of delivery |
| The cure | The date you treat as receipt, and what arrived during the window |
| The lease stack | Executed lease, amendments, guaranty, notice addresses |
| The space | Whether the tenant is operating, and a note of any property you can see without entering |

A sequence the source draft offered as a timeline — notice on day zero, five days running, filing on day six, a hearing inside two weeks, a writ inside three — is a sketch of an uncontested case. It is not your calendar. Counsel files. The court sets the date.

> Do not change the locks, remove property, or cut utilities to make a tenant leave. The source draft says Louisiana law prohibits self-help and that a landlord who does it can owe damages, including for lost business. Confirm the citation with counsel. Follow the prohibition now. A proper court process costs less than defending the shortcut.

## Eviction is a business choice after counsel says the path is open

Not every default should become a possession case. Attorney's fees, court costs, lost rent, restoration, and the cost to re-lease can exceed what you will collect. Retaining a tenant who is talking to you can be the better operating outcome. That comparison is yours. Whether you are allowed to terminate, to accept a payment plan without waiving the default, or to file is counsel's.

Use the scenarios as a discussion agenda, not as recommended legal action:

| What you are seeing | Options to price with counsel | The operating question |
|---|---|---|
| First miss, a long clean history, a documented hardship | A written workout, if counsel says it does not waive the default | Is the relationship worth more than a possession case? |
| Repeated lateness, one or two months outstanding | Notice and a demand counsel has approved; a possession filing if the cure expires unanswered | Is this a pattern the ledger already shows? |
| Several months outstanding and no response | Ask counsel whether a rule to vacate is available now | Are you spending staff time on a tenant who has stopped the conversation? |
| A non-monetary breach, rent otherwise current | Notice and cure as the lease states; a negotiated fix | The suite is occupied and paying. What does the breach cost the center? |
| A use the lease does not allow | Notice and a demand; a possession path if counsel says the cure failed | Who else in the center is affected, including exclusive-use neighbors? |

## Three communications, each reviewed before it is sent

The source draft describes an escalation in three steps. Keep the steps. Put counsel on each outbound letter.

1. **A courtesy note**, the day after the due date, saying the payment has not been received. This is a management signal. It is not the lease's formal notice unless counsel says it is.
2. **The formal notice of default**, in the method the lease requires, stating the amount or the facts, the cure, and any acceleration language counsel has approved. This is the letter that starts the clock you will later have to prove.
3. **A demand for possession**, only after counsel agrees the cure has expired without a cure, stating that you intend to file and on what grounds.

Every one of those letters can be an exhibit. A wrong amount, a wrong window, or a method the lease does not allow can end the sequence. The model may draft. A person checks the draft against the ledger and the clause. Counsel approves it before it is sent. "Where AI Helps with Contracts, and Where a Lawyer Still Has to Read It" puts default, termination, and non-renewal notices in counsel's column for this reason.

## Holdover is a clause, and a consent problem

A holdover is a tenant still in the space after the term, without a signed renewal. The source draft says that, in Louisiana, a commercial holdover with the landlord's consent is often treated as month-to-month at the same rent and on the same terms, and that a holdover without consent is a different status, exposed to a possession case and to holdover rent. Confirm both sentences with counsel before you accept a rent check after expiration or treat silence as consent.

Most commercial leases state a holdover premium. The source draft describes a common band of 150 to 200 percent of the base rent in effect at expiration. Your number is the percentage in that lease. The clause should also be read for whether holdover creates a month-to-month tenancy or a tenancy the landlord has not accepted. Accepting rent after expiration is a fact counsel needs to hear before you deposit the check.

## A surrender is a signed paper

When a tenant in default offers to leave, you can pursue possession or you can negotiate a surrender. A surrender, in the source draft, is a mutual termination: a vacate date, a condition standard, a treatment of the deposit, and a release whose carve-outs are written down. Outstanding rent stays outstanding unless the document compromises it for a stated sum that is actually paid.

Handshake surrenders are how the release becomes larger than you meant. Counsel drafts or reviews the surrender before anyone signs it and before you treat the space as yours. The deposit article's rule still applies: apply or refund only against the clause and the settlement, with the math on the log.

## Price the empty bay before you choose it

Unpaid rent is the visible cost. The rest is the vacancy. The source draft's ranges below are illustrative for a conversation about a small inline suite. They are not a budget for a named space, and they are not a Cypress Command property's experience.

| Cost | Illustrative range in the source draft | What changes it |
|---|---|---|
| Attorney's fees for the possession case | $2,500–$8,000 | A contest, an appeal, or a money claim joined to it |
| Court costs | $200–$500 | Filing and service in that court |
| Restoration | $5–$30 per square foot | The condition the tenant actually leaves |
| Lost rent | 6–18 months of base rent | The market, the suite, and the use you will accept |
| Re-leasing commission | 4–6% of total lease value | What you actually agree with the broker |
| Tenant improvement for the next tenant | $25–$65 per square foot | The next deal, not the last one |
| A sketched total for a 2,000 square foot inline | $50,000–$250,000 or more | Add the rows that apply; do not quote the total as a rule |

Put your own vacant-suite math on one page before you reject a workout. A payment plan that collects half of a stale balance can beat a possession case that returns a dark bay and a judgment you cannot collect. It can also teach the center that defaults are negotiable. Write down which of those you are choosing, and have counsel say whether the paper matches the choice.

## Questions to ask before you act

**What is the difference between the two defaults?** Monetary default is unpaid money. Non-monetary default is another broken promise. The notice and the cure usually differ. Copy both from the lease.

**May I change the locks?** No. Self-help is off the table. Counsel and a court order, or you do not touch the locks, the property, or the utilities.

**How long does a Louisiana commercial eviction take?** The source draft says about 21 to 30 days if uncontested, and 60 to 90 days or more if contested or appealed. Ask counsel what this court is doing now. Plan cash around a range.

**Should I take a partial payment?** Do not deposit it until counsel tells you whether acceptance changes the default or the right to pursue the balance. If counsel says to accept it, the receipt should say what it is and what it does not waive, in counsel's words.

**What happens to the deposit?** Apply it only as the lease and any settlement allow, against sums you can document, and return what remains on the timetable the lease states. Invoices and photographs support every deduction. The deposit article is the log.

**Can I still sue for rent after the tenant is out?** The source draft separates possession from the money claim: getting the space back does not, by itself, collect the arrears. A guaranty is a second document, useful only if counsel says it still reaches the debt. Bring both files.
