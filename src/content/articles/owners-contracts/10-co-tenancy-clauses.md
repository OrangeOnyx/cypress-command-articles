# Co-Tenancy Clauses: Reading the Trigger Before You Model the Haircut

> Context, not counsel. This article is not legal advice and it is not a substitute for a lawyer's reading of any lease. It describes how an owner abstracts co-tenancy language into a worksheet and an illustrative model. Whether a clause is enforceable, whether a trigger has occurred, and what notice or cure the document requires are questions for qualified counsel in the lease's jurisdiction. Louisiana leases, in particular, need a Louisiana lawyer before anyone relies on a day count, a remedy, or a waiver.

A co-tenancy clause conditions a tenant's rent, or sometimes its right to stay, on the continued presence, operation, or occupancy of other tenants in the same center. For the owner, that sentence is a contingent promise sitting inside an otherwise ordinary lease. One anchor departure can be the event that several other leases have been waiting for. The rent roll still looks whole until those remedies are read together.

This article is the reading for that clause family. It belongs with "The Clauses That Create Recurring Work: Notice, Renewal, Insurance, and Reporting" and with the seven-column obligation worksheet in "Every Contract Is an Operating Manual: Reading for Obligations, Not Just Terms." The numbers below are illustrative ranges used to show the shape of a model. They are not a market survey, not a cap-rate rule, and not a description of any Cypress Command property.

## Five shapes, one reading job

Retail leases use several co-tenancy shapes. The names vary. The reading does not. For each clause, copy four things in the lease's words: who must be open or present, what event counts, what the tenant may do, and how long the owner has before that remedy runs.

| Clause shape | What the text usually keys off | What the tenant may receive | What to flag for counsel |
|---|---|---|---|
| Anchor-dependent | A named anchor closes or goes dark | A rent reduction, often with a later termination right | Who counts as that anchor after a sale, a rename, or an assignment |
| Occupancy-based | Center occupancy falls below a stated floor | Percentage rent only, or another stated reduction | Whether "occupancy" means leased area, open-for-business area, or something else |
| Named co-tenant | A specific other tenant vacates | A flat reduction or an early termination right | Whether successors and assigns are inside the name |
| Radius | A competing store opens inside a stated distance | A reduction or a termination right | This is often its own clause family; counsel sorts it from co-tenancy |
| Dark | The anchor stops operating, including while it keeps paying rent | A reduction even though the anchor's rent is current | Whether remodeling, casualty, or a permitted closure is carved out |

A junior tenant's economic story is familiar: it took the space because a traffic-generating neighbor would be there. That story does not tell you what the signed page requires. Copy the page. Send the ambiguities to counsel with the section number attached.

## The trigger is the sentence that matters

The trigger is the condition that has to occur before a remedy is available. Two leases in the same center can describe "the anchor left" in ways that do not match. One may require the anchor to cease operating and to stop paying. Another may treat a dark, rent-paying anchor as enough. A third may start only after the tenant gives written notice, and may measure the cure period from receipt rather than from the closure.

Occupancy floors add a definition problem. A center can be 75 percent leased and 65 percent open for business on the same day. If the clause says "open and operating," the leased figure will not save you. If the clause says "leased," a dark suite that is still under lease may not count as vacant. An abstract that records only "70 percent" and drops the definition is not an abstract.

Common ambiguities to mark, not to decide:

| Trigger | Variants worth quoting | Question for counsel |
|---|---|---|
| Anchor closure | Vacated, dark, or ceased operations | Does a remodel, a casualty closure, or a permitted go-dark count? |
| Occupancy floor | Leased area versus open-for-business area | What date is occupancy measured, and who measures it? |
| Named tenant exit | A legal name, a trade name, or a use category | Do successors, assigns, and concept changes stay inside the name? |
| Cure clock | Notice date versus the event date | Is written notice required, and when is it effective? |
| Remedy switch | Automatic versus the tenant's election | Can continued payment, or a late notice, change the remedy? |

An open-air center makes a dark anchor visible from the street. Junior tenants will see the drop in foot traffic before anyone finishes reading the clause. Visibility is not the trigger. The trigger is the sentence you quoted.

## Three remedy shapes, and a model that stays labeled

When a trigger is treated as having occurred, the remedy in the lease is usually one of three shapes: a flat percentage cut to base rent, a conversion to percentage rent with no base-rent floor, or a termination right after a cure period. A center can carry all three at once. That is why a lease-by-lease reading understates the problem and a single spreadsheet cell overstates the certainty.

Flat reductions are common in older deals. The percentage is fixed in the lease. It does not scale with how much the tenant's sales actually moved. Percentage-rent conversions show up more often in later deals with national tenants. The tenant pays a percentage of gross sales and no base rent. If sales fell, the rent can fall hard. If sales did not, the reduction can be small, which is why this shape cannot be modeled without a sales figure you are allowed to use. Termination rights are a vacancy problem on a clock.

An illustrative worksheet, not a formula from any lease:

| Remedy in the lease | Illustrative range | What the model needs |
|---|---|---|
| Flat percentage reduction | Often described in the market as roughly 10–50% of base rent | The percentage in that lease, the base rent, and the months the reduction would run |
| Percentage rent only | Often described as a few percent of gross sales | The rate in that lease, and sales you can document |
| Termination right | After a cure period often described as 30–180 days | The cure length in that lease, and whether the tenant must elect |
| Sliding scale | Moves with the occupancy percentage | The occupancy definition, measured on the date the clause names |
| Abatement during cure | Base rent paused for the cure window | The window, and whether other charges pause with it |

If you need a scratch formula while counsel is still reading, keep it in the worksheet and label it illustrative:

- Haircut for one lease, flat reduction: base rent × the lease's reduction percentage × (cure months ÷ 12).
- Portfolio exposure, flat reductions: the sum, across leases, of base rent × that lease's reduction percentage × a probability you state as an assumption.
- Cascade, as a discussion figure: the anchor's own rent at risk, plus the reductions in the leases that name that anchor.

Replace every input with the figure from the signed page or with an assumption you can point to. Do not paste a "typical" percentage into a rent roll and treat it as the deal.

## Cascade is several leases sharing one name

Cascade is the pattern in which one anchor event is the trigger in more than one lease. The anchor's own rent is the visible loss. The other leases are the rest of the number. In centers where those clauses were never listed together, operators sometimes talk about a cascade multiplier — total income effect divided by the anchor's rent — in a wide illustrative band, sometimes described as a few times the anchor's rent. That band is a prompt to build the list. It is not a multiplier you may apply to a live rent roll.

Cure periods that start together are an operating problem even before anyone agrees they have started. If several leases recite a 90-day cure and the owner treats the same day as day one, the owner has one window in which to replace the anchor, negotiate waivers, or plan for the reductions. Replacing a large anchor inside a short cure window is often unrealistic. The calendar still belongs on the register, because the leases will not wait for a realistic replacement.

Illustrative scenario, invented for the arithmetic, not a Cypress Command center and not a tenant on any rent roll: a 70,000 square foot open-air center, one 18,000 square foot anchor at $180,000 a year, eight other tenants, and six leases that name that anchor with 25–40 percent reductions. The anchor is about 15 percent of a $1.2 million base-rent total. The six other leases, if every reduction ran for a full year at the low and high ends of that range, would be several hundred thousand dollars more. The point of the story is the ratio, not the dollars. Your center's ratio is the sum of the clauses you actually have.

## Abstract the stack, then let a person sign the abstract

Reading twenty co-tenancy clauses by hand is slow, and the spreadsheet goes stale at the next amendment. A model can ingest the PDFs, find candidate clauses, pull the variables, and draft a dependency list. "Where AI Helps with Contracts, and Where a Lawyer Still Has to Read It" already draws the line: the model prepares, a person checks the preparation against the signed page, and counsel reads meaning.

Use the same three jobs, in order.

1. **Extract.** Pull the clause and the variables, with page references: trigger, remedy, cure period, notice method, and the name the clause uses for the other tenant.
2. **Structure.** Put those variables in the obligation register, one row per lease, and build the list of leases that share a name.
3. **Quantify.** Run scenarios only after a person has checked the rows. Label every output as a model, with the assumptions written down.

| Phase | What the model may prepare | What a person confirms |
|---|---|---|
| Ingest | Searchable text with page references | The text matches the executed lease and its amendments |
| Find the clause | Candidate co-tenancy sections | No clause was skipped, including exhibits and side letters |
| Extract variables | A structured row per lease | Each field matches the quoted language |
| Map dependencies | A list of leases that share an anchor or co-tenant name | Trade-name variants and abbreviations were reconciled by a person |
| Model scenarios | An impact table under stated assumptions | The assumptions are written, and counsel has the rows that affect a position |
| Keep the trail | A citation for every extracted field | Someone signs the abstract before it is used |

The dependency list is the step that fails when leases are read one at a time. The same anchor can appear under a legal name, a trade name, and an abbreviation. The model can propose the matches. A person confirms them. Do not let a similarity score decide that two different tenants are one trigger.

## Three scenarios, and the outputs worth keeping

Once the rows are checked, three scenarios are enough to start a conversation: one named anchor leaves, an occupancy floor is breached under the definition in the leases, and every anchor-dependent clause is assumed to run at once. A probability-weighted blend is useful only if you can say where the probabilities came from. Otherwise report the scenarios separately.

Illustrative impact bands from the source draft of this article, for discussion, not a forecast:

| Scenario | Assumed condition | Illustrative share of base rent | Termination rights in the story |
|---|---|---|---|
| Base | No co-tenancy failure | None | None |
| One anchor leaves | The primary named anchor departs | Often sketched as 15–35% | A handful of leases |
| Occupancy floor | Occupancy under a stated floor, here 75% in the story | Often sketched as 20–40% | Several leases |
| Junior anchor leaves | A secondary anchor departs | Often sketched as 8–18% | One to a few leases |
| Full cascade | Every anchor-dependent clause is assumed to run | Often sketched as 35–60% | Many leases |

Outputs worth keeping in the file, each tied to the assumption that produced it:

- Maximum haircut in dollars under the full-cascade assumption.
- Any probability-weighted income figure, with the probabilities written beside it.
- A cascade note for each anchor: which leases name it.
- A cure-period calendar for clauses you are treating as active.
- A count of termination rights that would require counsel before anyone treats them as available.

## Ask for the limits before you sign

The cheapest time to limit cascade exposure is before execution. Modeling proposed language is an operating task. Changing the language is counsel's drafting, done with the business terms you actually want. Six positions show up often enough to put on the term sheet as questions, not as clauses you paste in:

| Position to raise | Why owners raise it |
|---|---|
| Substitution | Can a comparable replacement tenant cure the failure, and who decides "comparable"? |
| Cure length | Is the cure long enough to replace an anchor or to negotiate waivers, or does the remedy start while that work is still impossible? |
| Occupancy definition | Is the floor measured on leased area, or on open-for-business area? |
| Dark-and-paying | Does an anchor that is dark and current on rent sit inside the trigger, or outside it? |
| Named-tenant scope | Do successors, assigns, and a concept change keep the clause alive after a sale? |
| A center-wide cap | Is there a ceiling on reductions across leases, and where does that ceiling live so later leases cannot ignore it? |

A cap in one lease does not cap the others. A substitution right that counsel cannot recognize in the document is a talking point. Write the ask, then let counsel draft it.

## Four records, or you are guessing

Co-tenancy exposure moves. An anchor's health changes, occupancy changes, and a named tenant leaves. Four records keep the reading current.

| Record | What it contains | When it changes |
|---|---|---|
| Clause database | Every co-tenancy provision, structured, with citations | At execution and at each amendment |
| Trigger watch | Notes on anchor health and occupancy against the floors you copied | On a monthly pass, and sooner when a closure is rumored |
| Cure calendar | Start and end dates for any cure period you are treating as running | Weekly while a period is open |
| Waiver file | Any waiver that was actually signed, and what it covers | When a waiver is negotiated; counsel reviews it before it is sent |

A dashboard that is not tied to those four records is a picture of a spreadsheet. The register is the record. "The Obligation Register: One Table for Everything Your Contracts Require" is where the rows live beside the other dated promises.

## A metrics sheet for the file, not a benchmark

Use this sheet to see whether the abstract is finished. The ranges are the illustrative bands carried in the source draft. They are flags for a conversation, not underwriting rules.

| Metric | What to count | Illustrative band in the source draft | Question it should raise |
|---|---|---|---|
| Clause prevalence | Share of leases with a co-tenancy provision | Often sketched as 30–60% | Above that, do you have a stress test? |
| Anchor concentration | Anchor area as a share of center area | Often sketched as 15–35% | Above that, how many leases name that anchor? |
| Cascade multiple | Modeled income effect ÷ that anchor's rent | Often sketched as 2×–5× | Above that, is the model still using checked rows? |
| Average cure | Mean of the cure periods you copied | Often sketched as 60–120 days | Shorter than that, is the calendar already late? |
| Termination rights | Count of leases with a termination remedy | Your count | More than a few, counsel should see the list |
| Maximum haircut | Full-cascade assumption | Often sketched as 20–50% of base rent | Above that, the assumption needs a written owner |
| Probability-weighted income | Only if probabilities are stated | Often sketched as 5–15% under stated income | A larger cut is an assumption, not a fact |
| Occupancy floor | Average of the floors you copied | Often sketched as 70–80% of area | Below that, confirm you did not average away a tighter lease |

## Questions the file should answer

**Can a model abstract these clauses reliably?** It can draft the rows. Accuracy depends on the scan, the amendments, and the person who checks them. Use a row in an underwriting conversation only after that check. Use it in a notice, a waiver, or a dispute only after counsel has read the clause.

**How is a dark clause different from an anchor-departure clause?** In the dark shape, the anchor's failure to operate can be the trigger even while rent from the anchor is current. In a departure shape, the text may require the anchor to leave and to stop paying. Quote the verb. Do not import one definition into the other lease.

**How should this show up in a valuation?** As an assumption set beside income, and as a risk note beside the cap rate, only after the rows are checked. A specific basis-point premium is a judgment for the person signing the valuation, not a figure this article supplies.

**What is the first action?** Abstract every co-tenancy clause you have, including amendments, and build the list of shared names. Most owners cannot yet say how many clauses they have, which anchors those clauses name, or what the haircut would be under a written assumption. That list is the work. The model can speed the first pass. It cannot sign it.

## The practical next step

1. Pull the executed lease and every amendment for each suite. Confirm you are not reading a draft.
2. Add one register row per co-tenancy clause: source, who is named, trigger in the lease's words, remedy, cure period, notice method, and the internal owner of the row.
3. Mark every ambiguity in a column for counsel. Do not fill that column with your own answer.
4. Build the dependency list: which leases share a name, including trade-name variants a person has confirmed.
5. Run one illustrative scenario on the checked rows, label every assumption, and send the counsel column out as a single list before anyone treats a trigger as having occurred.
