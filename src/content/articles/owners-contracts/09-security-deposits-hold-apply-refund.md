# Security Deposits: Hold, Apply, and Refund Without Spending the Cash

A security deposit feels like rent that arrived early. It is not. Rent is income for a month of occupancy. A deposit is money you hold because a lease says you may, against reasons the lease names, until a moment the lease describes. Spend it on the landscaper and the liability is still there. The cash is not. That gap shows up when a tenant asks for the refund, when a buyer reads the balance sheet, or when someone "borrows" from the deposit for a tight week.

This article is the operating path for three movements: hold, apply, and refund. It is context, not counsel. Commercial deposit practice is not one national form, and residential rules are a different subject. Your leases, your accountant, and your lawyer decide what you must do. The standard below is how you avoid spending the cash while those answers sit in the file.

A deposit is not a roof reserve and not a sinking fund for common-area costs. "Budgets and Reserves" is about the owner's money for the building. This article is about the tenant's money in the landlord's hands.

## Income and a deposit are different postings

When the money arrives, the bank balance goes up either way. The books should not. Rent credits income. A deposit credits a liability, plainly named tenant deposits, until you apply it or return it. It does not sit in other income, and it does not reduce this month's repairs.

Even at one property the chart of accounts matters. If one bookkeeper codes a deposit to rental income and the next codes it to a liability, the income statement and the amount you owe tenants cannot both be right. Pick the liability line, write the rule beside it, and use it every time. "One Chart of Accounts, Many Locations" is the same point across sites.

An illustrative posting, not a record from any property: a new lease calls for $4,800 as a deposit and $2,400 as the first month's total rent. The tenant sends $7,200. Split it on the way in. $2,400 to rental income. $4,800 to tenant deposits. The bank shows $7,200. The income statement shows $2,400. One combined credit to income creates income you did not earn and hides money you may have to pay back.

A deposit that arrives before commencement is still a liability. Only a clause that applies it to a named month lets you move it, and then you move it in that month, with the clause cited on the entry.

## Read the clause before you touch the money

Part I of this series treats a contract as a list of obligations. A deposit clause is one of those obligations, and it usually answers five questions. Capture them on the obligation register, in the lease's words, not in your summary of what deposits "usually" do.

| Question | What to copy from the lease | Why the operator needs it |
|---|---|---|
| How much, from whom | The amount and the party | The liability starts at this number, not at what was convenient to collect |
| Where it sits, if the lease says | Any account or segregation language | This is a question for counsel if you are unsure what it requires |
| What it secures | The listed uses: unpaid rent, damage, other named items | You do not invent a use at move-out |
| What must be documented | Notices, invoices, inspection rights, as written | The file has to match the clause you rely on |
| When it comes back | The window, the condition, the payee | The clock starts from the event the lease names, which you record |

Silence in a lease is not permission to improvise a legal rule. Ask counsel how you will handle application and timing, write that answer down as the house practice, and follow it until the document or the advice changes. A deposit row with no owner on the obligation register is the row that gets spent.

Do not treat a last month's rent prepayment as a deposit unless the lease calls it one. Do not treat a deposit as last month's rent unless the lease says to apply it that way. Tenants often assume you will. The assumption is not the document.

## Three movements, and only three

Every dollar of deposit cash is in one of three states. Write the state on the row.

**Hold.** You received it, and you have not applied or returned it. The full amount is a liability. The cleaner operating default is a bank account used only for deposits, separate from the account that pays vendors and debt service. If counsel and your accountant say a separate account is required, that instruction controls. If they say one bank account is acceptable, the operating balance you are willing to spend still cannot dip into the deposit liability. Paying it back from next month's rent is spending it. The test is simple: cash set aside is at least the liability total. If it is not, fix that this week.

**Apply.** You reduce the liability because the lease allows a stated use and you have the paper. Illustrative uses, only when the document and counsel support them: unpaid rent that is actually unpaid, or damage beyond ordinary wear, with photos and invoices. The entry reduces the liability and credits the item you applied it to. The letter states the amount, the reason, what remains, and the section. It does not editorialize. Do not apply a deposit to an estimate you have not billed, a common-area true-up you have not issued, or a charge you cannot point to in the lease. Those may become real later. They are not a reason to drain the deposit because cash is tight.

**Refund.** Return what remains to the party entitled to it, inside the window you were told to use. Record the move-out date, the inspection date, the amount applied, the amount returned, the method, the payee, and the date sent. Keep proof of sending with the lease file. A refund reduces the liability and the deposit cash. It is not a negative rent entry. If you refund from the operating account because the deposit cash was short, the hold test already failed. Fix the shortage.

A $4,800 deposit with $900 applied and $3,900 returned is two documented movements and a liability that ends at zero. A liability that lingers after the refund means the books were never tied to the payment.

## The account and the ledger have to agree

Once a month, tie three figures. The liability by lease sums to the control total. Cash in the deposit account, or the restricted portion your accountant defined, covers that total. Each row still matches the lease amount, unless an application or a refund explains the difference.

One row per lease, on the obligation register or a log it points to. New deposits hit the log the day they arrive, already split from rent. Refunds hit the log the day they are sent. Applications hit the log when the letter goes out, invoices attached.

AI can list liabilities with no lease row, cash movements with no letter, and ended leases still on hold. A person decides whether an application is permitted and signs the refund. The model does not determine ordinary wear, and it does not say what the law requires you to return.

## The file that answers the question later

Buyers, lenders, and departing tenants ask the same three things. How much do you hold, where is it, and what happened to the amounts you no longer hold?

The file that answers is dull on purpose: the lease section on the register, the original receipt split between income and liability, the reconciliation your accountant accepts, and for every application or refund the letter, the backup, and proof of the cash movement. An open question to counsel stays open until counsel answers it. No account numbers and no last digits belong in the narrative log. The log has amounts, dates, parties, and document pointers.

The next owner will inherit the liability or expect it cleared before closing. An estoppel may ask the tenant to confirm the amount. Your number and the tenant's number should already match. That comes from keeping the three states current, not from reconstructing a year of mixed cash in a weekend.

### The view from Arnould Blvd

On The Blvd Shopping Center is one multi-tenant asset, about 63,000 SF and 27 units, at 101–149 Arnould Blvd. Each lease can carry its own deposit clause, its own amount, and its own clock. That is an obligation-register problem, the same shape as notice windows and insurance certificates elsewhere in this series. Command Platform holds the lease record those clauses live in. It is not, on this page, a description of any deposit account, any balance, or any tenant's money. Those facts stay off the public record. The operating lesson is the split: the cash that pays for the property and the cash a lease says you are holding are not the same cash, even when they arrive on the same day.

## The practical next step

1. List every lease that calls for a deposit. Copy the amount, the permitted uses, and the return timing onto the obligation register, and mark anything unclear as a question for counsel.
2. Compare that list to the liability on the books and to the cash set aside. Note every dollar of difference. Do not "plug" it to income.
3. Open a deposit account, or confirm in writing with your accountant why the one you have is the right place, and stop paying operating bills from deposit cash.
4. For the next move-out, use only hold, apply, and refund. Write the letter from the clause. Attach the invoices. Tie the liability to zero.
5. Add the three-way tie to the monthly close. A model may list the breaks. A person clears them.
