# The Clauses That Create Recurring Work: Notice, Renewal, Insurance, and Reporting

Most of a contract describes things that happen once: the price is set, the scope is defined, the parties sign. A small set of clauses keeps producing work for as long as the document runs, and sometimes after. This article names four of those clause families, shows what to capture from each, and gives you a recurring-work checklist to apply across your contract file.

## Why these four clauses

The first article in this series, "Every Contract Is an Operating Manual: Reading for Obligations, Not Just Terms," introduced a seven-column worksheet for reading any contract as a list of actions. This article narrows the focus. Across service agreements, supply contracts, leases, and subscriptions, four families of clauses tend to create the work that repeats: notice, renewal, insurance, and reporting.

They share three traits. Each one ties an action to a date or a trigger. Each one names a form the action must take, such as written, certified, or in an attached format. And each one is easy to satisfy in the first year, when everyone remembers the deal, and easy to miss in the third year, when the person who negotiated it has moved on.

Return to the illustrative landscaping company from the first article: a regional landscaping company with commercial and municipal service contracts. Its city parks agreement carries a notice clause, a renewal clause, an insurance clause, and a monthly reporting clause. So does its office park agreement, with different addresses, windows, and formats. So does the fleet lease it signed for its trucks, from the other side of the table. Twelve contracts with four recurring families each is close to fifty streams of recurring work, most of them small, all of them dated.

Reading these clauses well is an operating skill. Deciding what they mean legally, and whether a given action satisfies them, is counsel's work. The checklist below keeps those two jobs separate.

## Notice and renewal: the address and the clock

**Notice clauses** describe how the parties communicate formally. From each one, capture four things as the document states them: who receives notices, at what address, by what method, and with copies to whom. Then list the events elsewhere in the contract that call for notice, such as a default, a change of contact, a claim, or non-renewal.

The recurring work is maintenance. Addresses change on both sides. A customer moves its facilities office. Your company opens a new yard. The contract still names the old address until someone updates it in the way the document describes. A standing task to confirm notice details once a year, in both directions, costs little.

**Renewal clauses** describe what the document says happens at the end of a term. Capture the end date, what the text says occurs if no one acts, who must act to change that outcome, and the window for acting. The landscaping company's office park agreement might renew each year unless either party gives written notice within a stated window. Its snow addendum with the medical campus might instead require a new price proposal each fall.

Questions for counsel belong here more than anywhere. When does notice count as given? Does the method you actually use match the one the document names? How is the window counted? Write the questions down with the section number. Do not answer them from habit. The next article, "Dates That Run Themselves: Auto-Renewals, Notice Windows, and Evergreen Terms," covers how to put these windows on a calendar.

## Insurance and reporting: paper on someone else's schedule

**Insurance clauses** usually ask one or both parties to carry stated coverage and to show evidence of it. From each one, capture who must show evidence, what document is named, who receives it, and when it must be refreshed. The recurring work comes from a mismatch in schedules: a certificate follows the insurer's policy period, not the contract's term. A contract that runs three years may see three or four certificates, each one a small task for someone.

Whether a given certificate or policy satisfies the clause is a question for counsel and your insurance advisor. The operator's job is to know which clauses ask for evidence, where the current evidence sits, and when it lapses. "Certificates of Insurance: The Recurring Paper Most Owners Stop Checking," later in this series, takes up that tracking in detail.

**Reporting clauses** ask for information on a schedule: service logs, usage figures, inspection results, financial statements, incident reports. Capture the report, the frequency, the format, the recipient, and the due date. Reporting is the family most often left unpriced. A monthly service log in a customer's own format can take an office manager a day each month, and nobody counted that day when the price was set.

The same pattern shows up outside landscaping. An illustrative dental group might owe a monthly volume report under a supply agreement. A small manufacturer might owe quarterly quality data to a customer. The report is small. The habit of producing it on time is the recurring work.

## The recurring-work checklist

Run every contract through the same four rows. The checklist is a reading aid, not an interpretation.

| Clause family | Capture from the document | Recurring work it creates | Typical question for counsel |
|---|---|---|---|
| Notice | Recipient, address, method, copies, events that call for notice | Annual check of addresses in both directions; a log of notices sent and received | Does our actual method match the one named? |
| Renewal | End date, what the text says happens if no one acts, who acts, window | A decision date before each window opens; a record of the decision | How is the window counted? |
| Insurance | Who shows evidence, what document, to whom, refresh timing | Tracking each certificate's expiry and requesting replacements | Does this evidence meet the clause? |
| Reporting | Report, frequency, format, recipient, due date | Producing and sending each report; keeping a copy | Is a late or partial report treated differently? |

Apply it to the city parks agreement and a typical result looks like this, again illustrative: notice to the parks director with a copy to the city attorney's office; renewal by the city's written election before a stated date; certificates refreshed at each policy renewal and sent to the risk office; a service log by the 10th of each month in the city's format. Four families, four owners, four small calendars.

Add one more column when you use the checklist in practice: the internal owner. A family with no owner is the one most likely to drift.

## When the obligation outlives the document

Some documents state that certain provisions continue after the document itself ends. The phrase to look for is often some version of "shall survive termination." The operator's task is to find that language and record which provisions it names. What survival means for your business, and how long it lasts, is a question for counsel.

For the worksheet, a surviving provision gets its own row with its end date written as "does not end with the term, per the survival language in §X; confirm with counsel." That row stays on the list when the rest of the contract is archived. Confidentiality, records-retention, and indemnity provisions are common candidates in service agreements, and the landscaping company should expect to see some of them in its municipal paper.

### The view from Arnould Blvd

At On The Blvd, the church access-and-parking easement shows this pattern plainly. Section 3a carries a liquor waiver, and the easement states that the waiver survives termination of the easement itself. The resulting liquor line is drawn on the plat. That line is a recurring consideration: it bears on any prospective use that involves alcohol, and the easement's own text says it continues past the easement's life. For this series, the lesson is narrower: when you read for recurring work, read for the work that keeps going after the term ends.

## The practical next step

1. Choose the three contracts that generate the most work for your team and run each one through the four-row checklist.
2. Record notice details for every contract in one place, and set an annual task to confirm them in both directions.
3. List every reporting obligation with its format and due date, and note roughly how long each report takes to produce.
4. Search each document for survival language and give every surviving provision its own row that stays after the contract ends.
5. Send the questions from the last column to counsel as one list, each tied to its section.
