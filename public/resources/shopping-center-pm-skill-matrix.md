# Shopping Center PM Skill Matrix

A study frame for a working shopping-center operator. Score what you can show. This is operator context for a blank you fill in. It is not legal, tax, or investment advice. Where a lease, a lender, or a statute decides the next step, the worksheet records the question and the document. Counsel answers it.

A course certificate records study. The center is run from the work on this list.

ICSC, IREM, and CBRE publish education for shopping-center and commercial-property work. Their course pages are general context. The rows below are Cypress Command's operating list for an owner-led center.

---

## How to score

Each row is a whole step from 1 to 5. A higher step still includes the work at the lower steps.

| Step | What you can show |
|---|---|
| **1. Assigned** | Someone hands you the task. You finish the routine version. You can say what happened. |
| **2. Routine** | You handle the ordinary case alone. When the case is odd, you stop and hand it to the person who owns it. |
| **3. You own it** | The result is yours. You explain the month to the owner. You negotiate inside limits the owner already wrote down. |
| **4. You see it coming** | The calendar runs past this year. You act on a weak store, a cost dispute, or a renewal before it becomes a surprise. |
| **5. You set the standard** | You can show how the choice changes the property's value and the owner's hold. The next person uses your written standard. |

**The quarter.**

1. Score each row as a whole step. No half steps.
2. Circle the two rows with the widest gap between the step you can show today and the step this center needs.
3. For each circled row, produce one artifact: a reconciliation, a renewal memo, a capital list, a tenant handbook page, or a storm checklist you actually ran.
4. Score again in 90 days. The artifact is the score.

## Getting space leased

Leasing is how the center gets its income. The later sections protect that income.

| The work | 1. Assigned | 2. Routine | 3. You own it | 4. You see it coming | 5. You set the standard |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **The cash the deal produces** | You can point to base rent in the lease. | You can compute net effective rent on a simple term once free rent, the tenant-improvement allowance, and the commission are on the page. | You spread the allowance over the term, show free rent by month, and hand the owner the cash flow for the years they asked to see. | You put two or three structures next to each other and say which one fits this tenant's credit, this submarket, the hold, and the next refinance. | You can show what signing, or passing, does to value. The owner decides whether to sell. |
| **The path from terms to a signed lease** | You send the draft to counsel and you track that it went. | You can turn an approved term sheet into a letter of intent, and you can say where the draft sits. | You negotiate inside numbers and concessions the owner already approved. You keep a calendar from the letter to the signature. | You know the points this tenant marks up every time. The prior redline is your record of what was already traded. Large space and credit tenants go to counsel early. | The owner has written the limits the team may not cross. You run the list. |
| **Clauses that change the economics later** | You can find base rent, the term, and the use clause. | You can explain percentage rent, rent bumps, the tenant-improvement allowance, and an exclusive. | In the lease you hold, you can separate an opening co-tenancy condition from an ongoing remedy, and you can find radius, kick-out, go-dark, recapture, and assignment language. | When a tenant might go dark, you ask counsel four questions from that lease: must they keep operating, what notice is due, can the landlord take the space back, and what a dark store does to other tenants' rent. | You have lease language that has held up with more than one tenant. You can say what a clause does to the next deal. Counsel writes the sentence. |
| **Net, gross, or something between** | You know the three names. | You can say which costs move to the tenant under each one. | You pick a structure from the tenant in front of you, what similar space in this market uses, and whether your office can administer it. You can explain a net deal to a prospect. | You will offer a fixed shared-cost number when a careful tenant would otherwise audit every invoice. The trade is written down. | You look at recovery for the whole center. One rich deal that the rest of the leases cannot support is a weak choice. |
| **Which uses belong in the center** | You can name the tenants who are open. | You can name an obvious hole, such as no food, or a second shop in a use you already have. | You keep a written list of tenants you would call for the holes that matter at this center. | You compare vacant space with who actually shops the trade area. You use the mix to defend rent, and to keep the center off percentage rent in a single category. | A repositioning is a multi-year plan: which uses leave, which anchor you will pursue, and how the center trades during the gap. The owner approves the plan. |
| **Where the next tenant comes from** | You return broker calls. | You keep a broker list and you send what is actually vacant. | You also make outbound calls. A few brokers who know this center get your time. | You approach a national tenant through that tenant's real estate staff. ICSC events are one room where those conversations happen. | The owner has set the tour rules, what a broker is paid, and what you may promise in the market. |
| **Opening a renewal before the lease ends** | You send the notice the lease requires when the date arrives. | You start the conversation while there is still time to pursue a replacement if talks fail. | You open the file early enough to meet the lease's notice date, and you show the owner a keep-or-replace comparison: downtime, tenant improvements, and commission. | You can extend the term and average the rent into one payment, inside the owner's limits. You use a restructure to keep a credit tenant before the store is in trouble. | Across more than one property, you watch average remaining term, because a buyer or a lender will. |
| **What you trade to get a signature** | You give the point away to get the signature. | You hold the points the owner told you to hold. | You know what you will do if this tenant walks away. Every concession names what you received in return. | You trade rent dollars against other terms on purpose. More free rent can be the price of a guaranty. A narrower exclusive can be the price of a rent bump. | Co-anchors, joint ventures, master leases, and ground leases are counsel's documents. You attach the economics. The owner signs. |

## Tenants already in occupancy

A renewal you keep is commission and tenant-improvement money you do not spend again.

| The work | 1. Assigned | 2. Routine | 3. You own it | 4. You see it coming | 5. You set the standard |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **The week a tenant opens** | You hand over keys. | You confirm the certificate of occupancy, utilities in the tenant's name, the insurance certificate, and the sign permit. | The tenant leaves with a handbook, emergency numbers, how marketing works, portal access, and sales-reporting instructions if the lease requires sales. | After opening, you ask what failed, and you change the handbook. | The same opening list is what the next property uses. |
| **Walking the property on a schedule** | You go when someone complains. | You walk once a month. | You walk on a written schedule, you keep your own notes, and you speak with every tenant on a rotation you can sustain. | On the walk you look at stock, staffing, window signs, and traffic before the tenant calls you. | You can teach the next manager what you look for, on a walk you took together. |
| **Sales, occupancy cost, and a weakening store** | Sales reports are not in your file. | You collect sales when the lease requires them, and you file the report. | Occupancy cost is rent plus the other occupancy charges, divided by sales, for tenants who report sales. A high result is a reason to read the store. The owner sets the ratio that deserves a conversation at this center. | You read the store's result after rent. Thin inventory, fewer staff, and less advertising often show up before the rent is late. You confirm with the tenant before you act. | You use the pattern across a category when asking rents are reset and when a use is replaced. The owner approves the rents. |
| **Rent that is late** | You email a reminder. | You send the late fee the lease states. | You follow that lease: courtesy, the late fee it names, written default, and the cure it names. When the cure ends, counsel decides whether to file for possession. You do not borrow a day count from another property. | You ask counsel before you deposit rent after a default notice, because the deposit can waive the notice. You do not lock the tenant out. | Late-rent history is an input when you underwrite the next deal and when reserves are set. Counsel still owns the filing. |
| **A store that cannot pay** | You call counsel and wait. | You ask for the numbers the lease lets you see. | You write a deferral or a forbearance with a cure schedule. Counsel reviews it before anyone signs. | The choices sit on one page: defer rent, extend the term in exchange for the concession, take part of the space back, shrink the premises, move the tenant, or buy out the lease. You pick from credit, store results, and how hard the space is to re-lease. | The owner chooses between a workout and a termination with occupancy, and with a sale or refinance, in view. |
| **A tenant files bankruptcy** | You call counsel. | You can say that a debtor may assume or reject a lease. | You assemble the lease, the ledger, and the default notices. Counsel reads the current Bankruptcy Code for the clock and for any limit on a landlord's damages. You do not take a day count from this page. | You tell counsel which use, radius, and co-tenancy clauses the rest of the center depends on, so an assumption does not give those away. | You keep a list of which tenants would hurt the center if they filed, and the reserve the owner has set for that. |
| **An anchor goes dark** | You have not yet had to read a co-tenancy clause. | You know the lease has one. | You can separate a condition that applies before opening from a remedy that applies after an anchor or a named group leaves. You keep the list of who is on it. | Once a year you write which tenants can cut rent or leave if which anchor goes, and you name a replacement you would call. | New leases are written so one departure does not cut rent across the center. Counsel drafts the clause. You bring the tenant list. |
| **Short stays in the common area** | You have not booked one. | You have put a cart out once, on the paper that was handy. | A cart, a kiosk, or a short pop-up is a license with an end date and house rules, unless counsel tells you this one must be a lease. How it ends is written in the license. | A short stay can be a tryout for a longer lease. A sponsor in the common area is a separate check, and only where the existing leases allow it. | If short-term uses are a real income line, they have a budget line, a license form, and an owner who has seen both. |
| **Events the marketing clause allows** | Events are not on your calendar. | An event happens when someone suggests one. | You spend the marketing money the leases provide, on a calendar tied to when these tenants sell. | You write down whether the event moved traffic or sales, and you use that note when a tenant is deciding whether to stay. | The property has a program you can describe to a prospect with the results you measured. |

## The year-end cost bill

Shared-cost billing is where a single center quietly loses money.

| The work | 1. Assigned | 2. Routine | 3. You own it | 4. You see it coming | 5. You set the standard |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Which invoices the lease lets you bill** | You have read the shared-cost clause. | You can sort an ordinary invoice against that clause. | You can separate costs the lease lets you cap from costs it leaves outside the cap. Capital, financing, marketing, and anything the lease excludes stay out of the bill. | You keep a grid by lease, because the leases do not match. Each invoice is coded to a recoverable line when it is entered. | New leases use shared-cost language you can administer and defend. Counsel writes it. You say which lines caused last year's disputes. |
| **Each tenant's share of the area** | You bill the percentage already in the system. | You can divide the tenant's area by the center area. | You recompute the share from the lease. Area the lease leaves out stays out. Examples that appear in leases are an anchor pad the anchor owns, and an outparcel that does not share these costs. The lease in your hand controls. | If the center is redeveloped, you negotiate language so the denominator does not quietly shrink everyone's share. You recompute in any year the area changes. | Every property you report uses the same method, and you can explain it to an auditor. |
| **Caps on the increase** | You have not yet applied a cap. | You can find a cap clause and read the percentage. | You can tell whether unused room in a low year carries forward, and whether the ceiling compounds. You apply the clause that is in the lease when you reconcile. | When unused room carries forward, a later bill can rise by more than one year's step, up to the compounded ceiling. When the lease forfeits unused room, that room is gone. The lease names the base. Costs the lease puts outside the cap stay outside it. | Cap language on new leases is chosen so the center's recovery is explainable. A percentage from a different property stays in that property's file. |
| **Vacancy and variable costs** | You bill costs as incurred, with no adjustment for empty space. | You adjust the whole cost pool when the center is partly empty. | You adjust only costs that move with occupancy, and only to the occupancy the lease names. You write the method down. | Costs that stay the same whether the center is full or empty stay unadjusted. You can walk an auditor through the method. | The team uses one written method. New leases name the occupancy figure the same way. |
| **The fee on the bill** | A fee is not on the bill. | You add the fee the lease names. | An administrative fee on shared costs is a different charge from a management fee on income. You apply the administrative fee to the base the lease names. If the lease leaves taxes or insurance out of that base, they stay out. | You negotiate the fee inside the owner's limits, and you can show the tenant the lease sentence you are billing. | Fee language is consistent across the leases you control, and counsel has reviewed the form. |
| **Closing the year** | You send a year-end statement. | You close the books, compile actuals, compute each share, notify the tenant, and bill or credit the difference. | You send the package on the deadline in the lease, with the backup the lease requires. Disputes are answered in writing. | You know which tenants are likely to question the bill, and the backup for those lines is ready before they ask. | Next year's budget and the next lease negotiation use what this reconciliation showed. |
| **A tenant who audits the bill** | You gather what you can find. | You send the invoices you have. | You read the audit clause first. You provide the backup that clause requires. A finding is settled in writing. Years the clause does not open stay closed. | Each quarter you check the file you would hand an auditor. The year stays complete. | An organized audit becomes part of the renewal conversation, because the tenant has seen the backup. |
| **The gap between cost and what you billed** | You send the bill the software produced. | Once a year you compare recoverable cost with what you billed. | You read the gap line by line. You appeal a tax assessment when the numbers support an appeal and the owner agrees. | Invoices are coded when they arrive. A large repair is marked capital or operating from the lease and the invoice, before it is billed. Utility contracts are reviewed while you can still change them. | The owner has a recovery figure for the properties, and the monthly package shows whether you are inside it. |

## The building and the people who fix it

Maintenance you postpone becomes a capital bill.

| The work | 1. Assigned | 2. Routine | 3. You own it | 4. You see it coming | 5. You set the standard |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Work you schedule before it fails** | You repair what breaks. | You have a service contract on the HVAC. | You have a calendar for the systems that fail expensively: roof, rooftop HVAC, parking surface, striping, landscaping, site lighting, fire alarm, sprinklers, backflow, and the pylon sign. Intervals come from the vendor contract and from the site. On the Gulf Coast, humidity, storms, and salt air belong on that calendar. | Each system has a replacement year on the capital list. The year comes from an inspection or from the vendor, and the cost is in the budget. | The same calendar is the standard at the next property. A sensor or an alert is added when you can show it pays for itself. |
| **Sorting a repair request** | Requests arrive by text and email, with no order. | Requests sit in a work-order system. | You sort by what stops if you wait. Life safety and active water come first. A down HVAC unit in an occupied space comes next. Routine repairs follow. Planned work stays on the calendar. The response you promised is written down, including who is called if the first vendor does not answer. | Each month you report which promises you kept. Vendors who miss them come off the list. | Response times are the same across properties, and they sit next to tenant comments in the owner package. |
| **A vendor allowed on the site** | You hire the low bid. | You collect a W-9 and a certificate of insurance. | Before work starts you have the license the trade requires, a certificate naming the landlord as additional insured, a W-9, a signed agreement, and references you called. In Louisiana, confirm the contractor license with the state licensing board when the trade requires one. You read certificate dates every quarter. | Once a year you score vendors on price, quality, the response you were promised, and safety. | Preferred vendors are shared across properties, and the pricing reflects that volume. The owner sees the list. |
| **Rebidding a contract** | The contract renews itself. | You rebid when a price looks wrong. | Janitorial, security, landscape, and HVAC service are rebid on a cycle you wrote down, with at least three comparable bids. | An increase needs an itemized reason. Utility bills are read line by line. Service frequency follows the traffic you can document. | Portfolio contracts are negotiated from the volume you have, with counsel on the form. |
| **Capital the building will need** | You spend when something fails. | You have a capital budget for this year. | You keep a rolling list, at least five years, of major systems and a replacement year taken from an inspection or from the vendor. The reserve is the amount the owner and the lender agreed. | The reserve study is refreshed on the schedule the owner set. A capital cost is recovered the way the lease allows. A one-time bill to tenants requires a lease sentence that says so. Each project has a payback and an effect on income on the request. | The capital list is timed to a sale or a refinance. You recommend when to spend so the building matches the story a buyer or a lender will read. |
| **The first hour of a failure** | You call someone. | You have a call tree. | You have a written sequence for a leak, a break-in, and a fire, and you know where the storm binder is. Local government, utility, and law-enforcement numbers are for the jurisdiction you operate in. | You practice once a year. Tenants know how you will reach them. | The sequence is the standard across properties. A new manager can run the first hour from the binder. |
| **Landlord work and tenant work** | The invoice starts an argument. | You open the lease. | On a net lease, the roof, structure, and parking lot are usually the landlord's work, and the HVAC that serves only that tenant's space is often the tenant's. Water follows where it entered. You write the call down, and you check this lease before you send the bill. | You name the few disputes this property repeats, and you say them at move-in. | New leases use one set of maintenance sentences, reviewed by counsel, so the next call is shorter. |

## The report the owner reads

The owner trusts the operator who explains a variance before being asked.

| The work | 1. Assigned | 2. Routine | 3. You own it | 4. You see it coming | 5. You set the standard |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **The monthly package** | You export the report the software offers. | The package arrives on the agreed day and includes the rent roll and the late rent. | The package matches what this owner already expects: a short memo, the statement, budget against actual, the rent roll, aged receivables, leasing, capital, occupancy, and maintenance. The memo says what the month meant. Lines that moved have a reason and a next step. | The memo carries the month. The statements support it. | The same package is the standard for the team, and it is what you use when an asset decision is on the table. |
| **A rent roll that matches the leases** | You print the rent roll. | Once a month you check it against the leases. | Commencement dates, bumps, and shared-cost billings on the rent roll match the leases and the ledger. | A signed lease is abstracted into the system before the next charge that depends on it. You find the errors before a tenant does. | Every property uses the same abstract form. |
| **Next year's budget** | You take last year and add a round percent. | You build each line from last year's actuals. | Controllable lines are built from the work you will buy. Contracts follow the contract. Shared-cost billings follow this budget. | You show a base case and a downside: lost occupancy, one large tenant gone, and a jump in utilities. Space that is leased and not yet open is in the budget. | The budget ties to the owner's model and to the tests in the loan. You flag a capital choice that changes the hold. |
| **Income and what the property is worth** | Income and value are separate conversations. | You can state that value moves when income moves, at the cap rate the owner is using. | An illustrative check: removing $10,000 of recurring cost at a 7 percent cap rate is about $143,000 of value, because 10,000 ÷ 0.07 is about 143,000. Use the cap rate in the owner's model. | You can put a dollar figure on releasing at market, filling vacancy, cutting a cost, a capital project that lifts income, and closing a recovery gap. | The operating plan is dated to a sale or a refinance, and the owner has the effect on the hold. |
| **Numbers that change a decision** | You track occupancy. | You add late rent and the share of tenants who renew. | You and the owner keep a short list. Useful candidates are occupancy, sales per foot where the lease requires sales, occupancy cost where you have sales, renewals kept, late rent, income after expenses, tenant improvement and commission per foot, the gap between expiring rent and new rent, space leased but not open, the share of recoverable cost you billed, and capital against the plan. | You compare this center with its own prior year and with the budget. | The owner has targets. The monthly package shows them. |
| **Updating the forecast** | The budget stays in place all year. | You revise once, at mid-year. | You revise on a quarterly rolling view, and sooner when a lease, a capital job, or a cost changes the year. | A default, an insurance renewal, or a new tax bill updates the forecast in the same reporting cycle. | The cadence matches what the owner and the lender already require. |
| **The loan file** | You send what the lender asks for. | You send it by the date in the loan agreement. | You report the tests the loan actually uses, including coverage and occupancy when those are the tests, before a miss. | The budget shows a test that would fail two quarters out. The owner gets that note with time to decide on a refinance. | Lender contact for more than one loan sits with a person the owner named. The numbers they hear match the monthly package. |

## Questions you prepare for counsel

These rows are the file you bring. Counsel, the broker, or the CPA answers them.

| The work | 1. Assigned | 2. Routine | 3. You own it | 4. You see it coming | 5. You set the standard |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **The policies on the building** | You renew what you had. | You read the policies at renewal with the broker. | The stack matches this center: landlord general liability, umbrella, property, business interruption, equipment breakdown, and flood where the zone or the lender requires flood. You enforce the tenant minimums in the lease. A common commercial general liability form is $1 million per occurrence and $2 million aggregate. Umbrella and liquor limits are whatever the lease and the broker require. | Once a year the broker reviews premiums, limits, and gaps such as cyber, terrorism, and ordinance-and-law coverage. Certificates are checked as they expire. | Loss history is in the renewal meeting. Placements across properties are a broker assignment the owner approved. |
| **Getting into the center** | Access is not yet on your list. | You can find the accessible parking spaces. | You keep an accessible route from parking to an entrance, accessible restrooms, and van-accessible parking in the counts the standards require. The 2010 ADA Standards require at least 60 percent of public entrances to be accessible. You write down the date of the last look. | You schedule a periodic access review. For an existing center, counsel tells you which barriers the law expects removed when removal is readily achievable. Tenant-improvement work on a new deal includes the access work before the tenant opens. | A third party reviews access on a cycle the owner set, and the findings have a budget line. |
| **Safety on the job** | Safety sits with the contractor in your mind. | You know the owner can be responsible for hazards the owner controls. | Chemical labels, lockout when equipment is serviced, and fall protection for roof work are in the vendor agreement. | You walk safety on a schedule, you record incidents, and vendors hear the site rules before they start. | The same safety rules apply at each property. The owner sets the target. You report incidents against it. |
| **Permits in this parish** | You renew a permit when a notice arrives. | You track the certificate of occupancy. | You have the building official, the health department for food uses, and alcohol licensing where a tenant sells alcohol. Each tenant permit has a date. Office names differ by parish. | You hear about an ordinance change from the parish before it affects an opening. | Someone watches the same permits across properties and tells the owner when a rule would change a tenant's use. |
| **The environmental file** | The file has no environmental section. | You know a purchase can include a Phase I environmental site assessment. | You commission a Phase I when you buy and when the lender requires one. Tank registration and closure records stay with the state environmental agency if the site has or had tanks. Food tenants have a grease trap on a pump schedule. Refrigerant work is done by a technician allowed to do it. | After closing, you still watch tenant uses that store or spill chemicals. The file has inventories, a spill kit, and an incident log. | Environmental items sit on the capital list and in the sale file, so a buyer is not the first person to ask. |
| **Personal data in the office** | You have not listed what the office collects. | Passwords are unique, and the portal login is not shared. | You can list the personal data you hold, where it is stored, who can open it, and how long you keep it. Card numbers stay with the processor. Counsel tells you which Louisiana notice rule applies if data leaves the file. | The list is written. Vendors who hold the data have security terms in their contracts. The response steps are written before you need them. | The same data rules apply at each property, and the owner has seen them. |
| **Possession** | You call counsel. | You send a demand that matches the lease. | You can brief counsel on the notice and cure path in this lease, including what would waive notice. You do not lock the tenant out. You do not file, and you do not tell anyone else to file, from this matrix. | Notices go out in a form that a later rent check does not undo. Ask counsel before you deposit. The file is complete before counsel drafts. | The handoff to counsel is the same for every operator at the company. Default history informs the next lease. It is not a filing script. |
| **Storm season** | Last time, you boarded up when the storm was close. | You have a call tree. | Before the season you run a checklist. You have the days-before and morning-after sequence, a damage log, and tenant messages written in advance. After a flood claim, the proof-of-loss deadline comes from the broker, for the policy in force. | You practice yearly. Vendors who will respond have signed agreements before the season. The broker has the property schedule. | A new manager on the Gulf Coast can run the season from the binder you wrote. |

## How the work gets done

These show up inside the other rows. Score them on the same steps.

| The work | 1. Assigned | 2. Routine | 3. You own it | 4. You see it coming | 5. You set the standard |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **What you tell the owner** | You report when asked. | The monthly package is on time. | You lead with what changed and what can go wrong. | The owner asks you before making the decision. | More than one owner entity gets the same memo standard. |
| **How far ahead the calendar runs** | You plan the week. | You plan the quarter. | You plan the next twelve months. | You plan into the year after this one, and each task sets up the next. | The plan matches how long the owner intends to hold. |
| **The tenant's sales** | The tenant is the payer on the ledger. | The tenant is also a customer of the property. | If their sales hold, a renewal is easier and the rent has somewhere to go. | When a category is weak, you change operations and deal terms inside the owner's limits. | A prospect can hear how the open tenants perform, from numbers you are allowed to share. |
| **The systems you already pay for** | The software holds the rent roll. | Work orders live in the system too. | The monthly numbers are visible without a scavenger hunt. Where you have them, you read traffic, sales, and occupancy cost together. | You add a tool, including an automated one, when the payback is written down: invoice coding, lease abstracts, or a maintenance alert. | Software decisions are scored against income. The owner approves the spend. |
| **Utility cost and the lease** | Lamps are changed when they fail. | You track the utility bills. | You know whether the leases let you share meter data or recover an efficiency project. | An efficiency upgrade is proposed with the lease path for recovering the cost, and with the effect on the utility line. | Tax credits or green financing go to the CPA and the lender with the project memo. |
| **A decision the list does not cover** | You wait to be told. | You follow the written list. | You pick which list fits the file in front of you. | When no list fits, you write the reason for the choice you made. | The next person uses that note. |

## Where to spend the next quarter

Score the rows, then pick the thin ones. On a single center, the year-end cost bill, a bankruptcy file, and the environmental records are often the thin spots, because those files stay quiet until they are not. Leasing and repairs are often further along, because they happen every month.

Five artifacts, in an order that pays off:

1. One shared-cost reconciliation built from the leases at this center. The workbook and the letter to the tenant are the artifact.
2. A keep-or-replace memo on the next renewal. Compute net effective rent from the term sheet you have. Show downtime, tenant improvements, and commission on a replacement.
3. A late-rent file you walk with counsel. Read the notice and cure clauses in that lease. Counsel decides whether anyone files. This matrix is not a filing guide.
4. A capital list for this center. Each major system gets a replacement year from an inspection or from the vendor who services it. The reserve is the amount the owner and the lender already agreed.
5. A monthly package the owner can read without calling you. Explain the lines that moved.

IREM and BOMA publish courses for people who manage commercial buildings. Read the current page for what a course covers and what it requires before you treat a brochure as a promise.

Check ICSC's current [education pages](https://www.icsc.com/attend-and-learn/professional-development/education/existing-credential-designations) for which credentials are still offered; the published exam specifications are useful reading lists either way.
